# Chapter 1 — Scene 2

Aetherfall: Genesis did not open with a menu.

It gave me feet.

One second I was in my apartment with plastic cuffs around my wrists and the taste of room-temperature energy drink doing structural damage to my faith in civilization. The next, my boots were planted on black stone veined with blue light, and my body belonged to the better-funded version of me.

Not different.

Improved.

That was the thing Aetherfall had gotten right before anyone else. Most VR games made avatars that looked like ambition wearing skin. Taller, sharper, more symmetrical, the jawline of a man who had never debugged anything at two in the morning. Aetherfall started with a full-body scan and then gave it the mercy of art direction.

Photorealistic, but tuned.

Cleaner motion. Better posture. Skin that did not remember every bad week.

The game looked at you and said: yes, this is you; no, we do not need to be cruel about it.

My reflection waited in the polished stone beneath me: shaved head, broad shoulders, brown eyes, face still mine even with the slight heroic correction the renderer applied to everyone. Aetherfall had decided my cheekbones needed hope. I had declined to argue with the machine.

The 404 lobby loaded around me.

We were in a prep vault beneath the match arena, one of the neutral spaces the game used before ranked scrims: black stone floor, floating terrain map, thirty spawn sigils on our side, thirty dark sigils across the dividing line for Dead Hand. Above the map, the match clock counted down from seventeen minutes.

LOGOUT sat in the upper-right corner of my vision, blue and available.

Below it:

PAIN FEEDBACK: TOURNAMENT STANDARD
HAPTIC CAP: ACTIVE
MATCH AUTHORITY: GUILD LEADERS + SYSTEM

Normal facts.

Boring facts.

The kind of facts you noticed once and then forgot because floors did not need applause for continuing to be floors.

The arena model rotated over the table.

Stormfall Basilica.

Broken terraces. A dry canal splitting the middle lane. Two ruined bridges. Collapsed arches. Half-standing columns. A left-of-center basin where the match boss waited, curled around its own brass heart like something old enough to resent being made into content.

This season’s center objective was the Storm Warden: a stone-and-root giant folded against the left side of the basin, one buried hand sunk into the arena floor and the other gripping a cracked pillar. Brass ribs sealed its storm core. Rootwork bound its shoulders into the broken temple floor. Under normal rules, it stayed dormant until a team damaged it, claimed the basin, or let the objective timer wake it.

Once active, it had a cyclone phase that dragged loose players toward the center and punished anyone who tried to fight the map like it was flat.

Dead Hand usually treated boss mechanics as decoration someone else had paid for.

Tonight, so did we.

Brent stood by the map with both arms crossed, healer’s mantle hanging off his shoulders in layered gray-white cloth. He always looked slightly annoyed in support gear, as if the robe had made a claim about his personality that he intended to dispute in writing.

Mathias was three steps back from the table, not looking at the map so much as watching how everyone used it. Strider leathers, short cloak, compact bow slung low. Not a stationary sniper. Not a pet hunter. Mathias fought routes. He found lines through places the enemy thought were closed, took one precise shot, then relocated before the answer arrived.

His eyes moved from the spawn sigils to the Dead Hand side, to our voice roster, then back to the empty space at the head of the table.

Seb’s space.

Empty.

That landed wrong before I had a reason for it.

Seb was not always first into a lobby. He had a life, a job, a brother whose medical situation had converted half his schedule into logistics, and a talent for convincing the rest of us that crisis management was just another form of cardio. But he was never absent from the part where a team became a team.

He liked the last ten minutes.

Status checks. Roles. One objection, if anyone had one. Then the little shift in his voice where thirty people who had been individuals five seconds earlier became one structure with thirty moving parts.

The head of the table stayed empty.

“Tell me he’s invisible,” I said.

Brent glanced over. “He’s late.”

“I was asking for the comforting lie first.”

“You logged into the wrong support class for that.”

Mathias’s mouth moved like he almost smiled and then decided to keep the energy for something useful. “One message. Family thing. On his way.”

“How long ago?”

“Eight minutes.”

That was worse.

Eight minutes was not a delay. Eight minutes was a choice that had failed to finish becoming an explanation.

Kip crossed the near side of the table, already armored, already restless, glaive spinning once across his palm before he caught it and planted the butt against the stone. Kip liked forward pressure the way some people liked coffee. Necessary. Immediate. Morally clarifying.

“We can still run the opening without him,” Kip said. “Dead Hand won’t wait because our leader had a family thing.”

“They’re waiting too,” Mathias said.

On Dead Hand’s side, twenty-nine spawn sigils had lit.

One remained dark.

Their leader stood opposite us, avatar half-armored in black and brass, face uncovered, expression blank in the practiced way elite players used when cameras might be somewhere. Dead Hand had the posture of a team that did not need to fill silence to prove it was comfortable inside it.

Their leader opened cross-team comms.

“One missing on our end,” he said. “Rogue. Thirty seconds, maybe less.”

Kip made a low sound. “Convenient.”

Brent looked at him. “Our leader is missing.”

“I said convenient, not unique.”

Dead Hand’s leader did not react. “We can reschedule if you need your caller.”

That was not courtesy.

It was a blade with manners.

Mathias looked toward me. Not asking me to answer. Just marking the route available.

I opened my mouth.

Seb arrived.

His spawn sigil flared gold-white at the head of the table, and Seb Rainier stepped into the lobby already moving. Tall, composed, Warlord armor resolving around him in layered dark metal and red cloth. His avatar had given his real face the same high-fidelity polish as the rest of ours, but it had not softened what mattered: the calm eyes, the broad shoulders, the presence that made rooms sort themselves around him before anyone voted.

Usually, Seb brought warmth with him.

Not softness.

Warmth.

Tonight the warmth arrived half a second late.

“Sorry,” he said. “Family thing. I’m here.”

No one asked.

That was how you knew everyone wanted to.

Seb did not give the room time to become a place where questions could live.

“Status.”

Brent answered first. “Dead Hand has one missing. Rogue.”

“Same opening?” Seb asked.

“Unless you want to invent confidence on the way down,” Brent said.

“Tempting. Inefficient.” Seb turned to the map and expanded the left-of-center basin with two fingers. “Storm Warden stays asleep unless touched, claimed, or timed awake. We don’t touch it. They won’t either unless we make killing us too expensive. First three minutes are players, not boss.”

There he was.

Mostly.

The cadence was right. The logic was right. But the words came too clean, like he was reading them off a surface he had built between himself and the room.

“Brent, center pocket. You do not chase saves outside pocket unless I call it.”

“Good. I enjoy not dying for stupid reasons.”

“Two defenders tight on Brent. Your job is being the bodies their rogue has to solve before she reaches him.”

Two avatars near Brent lifted weapons in acknowledgment. Defensive builds. Not famous. Not faceless, either. People who had practiced a job that would only become visible if they failed it.

“Kip, forward pressure but not full commit. Make them answer you. Do not go sightseeing.”

Kip grinned. “That happened one time.”

“It happened memorably.”

A few people laughed.

Not much.

Enough to prove the shape still worked.

“Mathias.”

“Routes, rotations, punish anyone who thinks a wall means safe.”

“And their control?”

“I’ll make her keep spending.”

Seb nodded once. “Marcus.”

“Adaptive denial, emergency mistake-correction, occasional moral support.”

“Drop the last one.”

“I can’t. It’s load-bearing.”

That got the smallest real smile out of him.

For half a second, I believed the room might settle.

Then the last Dead Hand sigil lit.

The rogue arrived without flourish.

A hooded avatar stepped into the far end of the lobby in black leather armor cut close to the body, red accents worked through the seams and bracers. A fitted black mask covered the lower half of her face. The hood shadowed her eyes without hiding them completely: green, direct, already moving across the room. Twin short swords crossed over her back. A red braid slipped forward from beneath the hood and lay against the black armor like a warning someone had chosen not to conceal.

She took in the room once, turned to her leader, and shifted two steps left into a gap I had not noticed until she occupied it.

Dead Hand changed around her.

Not dramatically. That would have been easier to dismiss. Their front line angled a few degrees. Their support stopped looking at the boss map and started looking at escape lanes. A glacial mage behind the rogue lifted one hand, and pale frost gathered around her fingers.

One player logged in late.

The match became heavier.

“Rogue online,” Brent said.

“Yes,” Mathias said softly. “That does seem to be the room’s opinion.”

My model had called her the red-line threat.

Public data agreed. Casters agreed. Everyone who had lost a healer to Dead Hand agreed, usually in language broadcasters could not use.

Dead Hand’s assassin.

The match clock hit ten seconds.

Seb’s voice dropped into command.

“Remember what we’re testing. Not whether we can out-objective them. Whether we can survive when they decide the objective is us. We know enough. Once we move, we move.”

The arena opened under our feet.

Falling in Aetherfall never felt like falling. The transition took your stomach out of the problem. The vault dissolved, the match server caught thirty bodies per side, and reality rebuilt itself around the selected map.

Broken sky.

Black stone.

A ruined storm-temple spread across a wide basin of cracked bridges, fallen arches, and torn banners. The Storm Warden slept left of center, folded against the sunken stone with its brass ribs sealed over a dark core. One root-bound hand disappeared into the floor. The other clutched a pillar as if it had fallen asleep holding the arena together.

No one damaged it.

No one claimed the basin.

No one woke the thing that could turn half the map into a cyclone.

Thirty green markers spread across my HUD.

Thirty red.

Boss timer. Shrine timers. Team resources. My Aspect meter at full.

LOGOUT remained blue.

The floor was still the floor.

“Move,” Seb said.

404 moved.

We took the west approach in a staggered wedge: Kip and two brawlers forward, Brent in the center pocket with his two defenders tight on either side, Mathias already peeling high along the broken terrace. I stayed one lane behind the main pocket with my Aspect suppressed.

Across the basin, Dead Hand ignored the Warden and cut toward the canal bridges. Their front line made noise there on purpose. Not a real claim. A tax. Respect this lane, answer this pressure, spend a little attention where they wanted us to spend it.

Their rogue and glacial mage angled off the main push together.

The rogue did not need another assassin beside her.

She needed the ground, the sightlines, and the timing to stop cooperating with the people trying to protect her target.

“Left bridge pressure,” Mathias said. “Not full. They want us to respect it.”

“Respect it politely,” Seb said. “Do not marry it.”

Kip’s group hit first.

Metal and spell-light collided under the broken archway. It looked, for twelve seconds, like a normal elite scrim: feints, cooldown trades, shield pressure, a stun burned early and punished, two ranged players trying to win a sightline neither could hold.

The Warden shifted in the basin, its brass core pulsing once, warning anyone near the center that the objective existed and had opinions.

Everyone ignored it harder.

Then Dead Hand’s rogue moved.

The first step was not toward Brent.

That mattered.

She cut toward Kip’s pressure group first, low and outside, forcing Brent’s right defender to widen half a step so the pocket would not get split if Kip got collapsed. The correction was right. That was the unpleasant part.

The glacial mage placed frost under the defender’s recovery foot as the step completed. Thin. Nearly transparent. Not a root. Not a stun. Just enough slickness to turn the next step into a decision.

The defender had to look down.

The rogue used the look.

“She’s not on Kip,” Mathias said. “Brent.”

“Hold pocket,” Seb snapped.

I triggered Sentinel.

Blue light built around me before the armor finished locking in: shoulder plates, bracers, a heavy chest shell, and a broad aegis of hard-light geometry unfolding from my forearm. The shield was not metal. It was layered force, anchored through my wrist, elbow, and shoulder.

I put it between the hooded rogue and Brent.

She came in low.

One short sword cut toward my lead leg.

I dropped the shield and hardened it.

The blade never hit.

Serra turned sideways before contact, planted both boots on the blue lattice, and used my own answer as a step.

She went up my shield, over my shoulder, and past me.

For one stupid half beat I was still braced against the strike she had never meant to land.

Her first sword went in under the ribs.
