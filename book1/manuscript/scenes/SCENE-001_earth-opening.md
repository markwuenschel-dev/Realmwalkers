# Chapter 1 — Scene 1

## June 27th, 2040

## Charlotte, North Carolina

Breaking news crawled across the bottom of the television while my apartment pretended it was a place where a balanced adult lived.

The counters were clean. The floor had been vacuumed within the last geological age. There were no dishes in the sink, which felt less like evidence of discipline and more like evidence that no one else had been around to create a second opinion.

On the coffee table, my laptop showed a progress bar stuck at eighty-seven percent.

It had been stuck there for eleven minutes.

That was the thing about machine learning. Everyone wanted to talk about artificial intelligence like it was a newborn god learning to dream in silicon, but most of the job was watching expensive math sit motionless while a log file refused to admit what it had done wrong.

I nudged the laptop with my toe.

The progress bar remained unimpressed.

“Coward,” I told it.

The television anchor kept smiling.

“Tonight, Astria is preparing to unveil what company leadership is calling a once-in-a-generation breakthrough in quantum-assisted biomedical computing. Executives say the platform may redefine clinical research, predictive medicine, and human-computer integration.”

Human-computer integration.

That phrase made me look up.

Not because it was surprising. Astria generated phrases like that the way certain trees generated pollen: seasonally, aggressively, and with total confidence that everyone else should breathe it in. Still, there were grades of corporate language. Some phrases came from marketing. Some came from legal. Some came from a meeting where marketing had used too many verbs and legal had quietly taken the good ones outside and drowned them.

Human-computer integration sounded like the third kind.

The screen cut to a slow aerial shot of Astria’s main campus somewhere out west: glass buildings, solar fields, walking paths designed by people who believed innovation increased if the shrubbery was expensive enough. A lower-third banner identified the CEO, Richard LeBlanc, though the footage only showed him from a distance, crossing a lobby in a tailored suit with three people trying to keep up.

“Sources inside the company say tonight’s announcement follows years of development under strict internal security.”

That was true enough.

Strict internal security was one way to describe Astria. Another was that the company treated information like a prescription narcotic: every milligram tracked, every access logged, every deviation followed by a polite meeting with someone whose smile had never reached their eyes.

I worked there, technically.

Full-stack machine-learning engineer was the closest clean label, if you were the kind of person who believed labels stayed clean after touching actual work. In practice, I built the connective tissue between models, data pipelines, internal tools, dashboards, and the people who needed answers yesterday but had not yet decided what question they were asking.

Some of it mattered.

That was the annoying part.

Clinical decision support, adverse-event prediction, image triage, patient-chart summarization. Real tools. Real doctors. Real patients somewhere downstream of all the versioning, validation, and compliance meetings. I believed in that work more than was probably healthy for a person who also knew exactly how many slides could be added to a project before the project stopped being real.

Astria did good work.

Astria also described good work in a way that made me want to hide under furniture until the press release passed.

The anchor’s voice warmed half a degree, which meant we had entered the reverence portion of the broadcast.

“Analysts are already calling the announcement a possible turning point for both biotech and advanced computing. With quantum acceleration, Astria says its newest system could process biological complexity at a scale previously thought impractical.”

Previously thought impractical.

I glanced back down at my laptop.

Eighty-seven percent.

“Tell that to the random forest currently dying of stage fright in my living room.”

The project had nothing to do with Astria. Not officially, not unofficially, not in any of the interestingly illegal middle categories people pretended did not exist until discovery. It was mine: a personal model trained on public match data from *Aetherfall: Genesis*, trying to predict when elite teams abandoned boss pressure and pivoted into player-kill routes.

The kind of thing normal people did not build on a Friday night.

The kind of thing that might have given 404: Aim Not Found a half-percent edge against Dead Hand.

Half a percent mattered. At Worlds, half a percent could be the difference between a respectable quarterfinal exit and the kind of run that changed the next five years of your life.

I had a job that paid well enough to keep my apartment quiet and my equipment current. I had a team that had clawed into the world bracket by the edge of its fingernails. I had a calendar full of scrims, reviews, build deadlines, model checks, patch notes, code reviews, and reminders to do things like buy more coffee before the absence became an emergency.

What I did not have was a life that made much noise when the machines were off.

The television filled the room because silence had started making eye contact.

“LeBlanc is expected to speak at the top of the hour,” the anchor said. “Astria has teased the device as a breakthrough that could, in the company’s own words, ‘alter life as we know it.’”

I stopped with one hand inside the laundry basket.

There it was.

Alter life as we know it.

A sentence like that should have required a permit.

No one who actually understood a technology talked that way unless someone with a media strategy had removed all the load-bearing nouns. Real breakthroughs were ugly up close. They had error bars, heat problems, bad edge cases, undocumented failure modes, and one exhausted engineer in a chair somewhere whispering, why are you like this, to a process that had passed every test until it met reality.

“Alter life as we know it,” I said.

The apartment gave me nothing.

The socks remained divided by color and indifference.

I matched two black ones that were almost certainly not from the same manufacturing decade and dropped them onto the couch.

My phone buzzed on the armrest.

For one second, something in my chest performed the stupid little lift it had been performing for ten days, which was embarrassing because I was thirty-one and should have been immune to my own nervous system by now.

It was not her.

Mathias:
Lobby in twenty. Your model can be wrong in the lobby too.

Then, a second later:

Brent:
If he’s late, we tell Dead Hand he got eaten by his own spreadsheet.

I smiled despite myself.

The thread above their messages showed another name in my recent DMs. No unread dot. No convenient little red badge giving me permission to treat it like an obligation.

Serra Hawthorne.

I had not reopened it in two days.

Not because I did not want to. Wanting was not the issue. Wanting was sitting there like an unsecured cable across a dark hallway, waiting for me to pretend I had not already tripped over it twice.

The preview did not ask me anything. That was the problem.

I turned the phone facedown.

Work asked for deliverables. Games asked for decisions. Serra, somehow, had managed to ask for neither and still occupy more processing power than both.

That was inefficient.

A useful observation, if I ever planned to do anything with it.

I did not.

The laptop chimed.

The model finished.

I leaned over the coffee table and scanned the output. Prediction confidence, feature weights, a messy little visualization of team behavior clustered by objective timing. Dead Hand occupied the upper-right quadrant where all unpleasant things lived: high aggression, low reset frequency, fast punishment on support exposure.

No surprise there.

Their assassin-mage tandem was the point. Everyone knew about the red-line threat, the star rogue who turned support players into spectator icons and made casters develop religious feelings about line of sight. There was also the other problem Mathias kept complaining about, the one public match data never quite explained. A second pressure source. A missing shape. Dead Hand won too many fights where the visible assassin had not done enough visible work to account for the collapse.

The second threat did not look like another knife in the dark. It looked like the battlefield becoming less cooperative whenever the rogue committed. Lanes narrowed at the wrong time. Retreat paths turned expensive. A support player took one clean step backward and found the space already claimed by ice, mist, wall, slow, or whatever flavor Dead Hand used to make geometry hate you.

She did not make the rogue dangerous. The rogue was dangerous all by herself. The mage made the right answer obvious for her and expensive for everyone else.

Mathias called it missing weather.

I hated how accurate that felt.

My model did not hate anything. It merely produced a confidence score of 0.71 and waited to be praised for its restraint.

“Seventy-one percent,” I said. “Bold of you to have no spine in my time of need.”

Still, the output was useful. It supported the plan we wanted to test tonight: force Dead Hand into a player-kill route, make them spend their best pressure early, and see whether my Aspect build could break their assumptions before they broke Brent.

Brent was going to hate that phrasing.

Brent hated most phrasing that took longer than necessary to say who was getting hit.

I saved the run, pushed the notes into the team folder, and checked the time.

Nineteen minutes.

Enough time to eat something, if I had already made better choices earlier. I opened the freezer and considered the available categories of regret.

One frozen dinner with a sodium content that felt legally ambitious.

One bag of mixed vegetables I had purchased during a brief period of optimism.

Two ice packs.

No actual meal.

I closed the freezer.

“Strong household management, Marcus.”

The energy drink on my desk was already open. It had become room temperature, which made it less a beverage and more a dare.

I took the dare.

The first swallow tasted like carbonated battery acid and artificial blue, though not necessarily in that order. My body accepted it with the weary professionalism of an organization that had given up trying to improve leadership.

The television shifted again. A panel of analysts had appeared, which meant three people in expensive clothes were about to speculate responsibly.

“This is not only a biotech story,” one of them said. “This is a market story, a national competitiveness story, potentially even a defense story.”

Of course it was.

Everything became a defense story if enough money stood nearby.

Another analyst leaned in. “And the privacy implications could be enormous. Astria has repeatedly stated that its systems are secure by design, but we are talking about biological data, predictive cognition, possibly neural-adjacent interfaces if the rumors are accurate—”

The host cut in smoothly. “Astria has not confirmed any neural interface component.”

“No, they have not. But several patent filings suggest—”

I muted the television.

The apartment settled.

That was better and worse.

Onscreen, the analysts kept moving their mouths around the future.

My headset sat on the desk beside the haptic cuffs, the neural feedback band, and the motion gloves. The rig took up too much of the room, objectively. So did the desk. So did the second monitor. So did the chair I had modified three times because commercial emulator chairs were designed by people who believed the human spine was a rumor.

It was a ridiculous amount of hardware for a hobby.

It was also not a hobby.

That distinction mattered to exactly no one outside the people who had spent years turning play into practice until the practice started looking like work and the work still did not guarantee anything. *Aetherfall: Genesis* was the biggest competitive VR title in the world, a hybrid mess of roleplaying systems, faction warfare, boss encounters, and team-based arena violence that should not have worked as an esport and somehow did.

Worlds started in July.

404 had qualified twenty-fifth.

Final slot.

No one outside our channel needed to know exactly how narrow that had been.

My phone buzzed again.

Mathias:
You alive?

I picked it up.

Me:
Define alive in a way that doesn’t discriminate against caffeine-based organisms.

Mathias:
Lobby. Fifteen.

Me:
I understand linear time.

Mathias:
Historically uneven evidence.

The Serra thread sat above the keyboard.

I looked at it too long.

Then I locked the phone and set it facedown again.

There were choices you made because they were right, choices you made because they were easy, and choices you postponed until they grew teeth and selected a category for you.

I was becoming familiar with the third kind.

The television, still muted, showed Richard LeBlanc stepping up to a podium. The caption beneath him read:

ASTRIA CEO TO UNVEIL “LIFE-ALTERING” QUANTUM PLATFORM

Life-altering.

There it was again, cleaned up for broadcast.

I watched him smile at the cameras. Not a villain’s smile. That would have been convenient. He looked tired, polished, and deeply convinced that history would forgive whatever the middle steps looked like.

I had seen that expression at Astria before. Not always on executives. Sometimes on researchers. Sometimes on engineers. Sometimes in the mirror after a sixteen-hour day, when the thing finally worked and the cost of making it work felt like a detail for a later version of yourself to audit.

My laptop fan spun down.

The room felt too still.

For a moment I had the unpleasant sense that I was standing at the edge of several things I had not followed far enough: the announcement on the screen, the read thread on my phone, the model output waiting in the team folder, the company whose badge sat in my backpack by the door.

Then the team-channel invite pulsed again.

Not a message this time. Just the call notification, waiting.

I exhaled.

“One impossible system failure at a time.”

I turned off the television before LeBlanc could start talking.

The screen went black, leaving my reflection in it: shaved head, tired eyes, old T-shirt, shoulders a little too tense for a man about to play a game. Behind me, the apartment looked clean and temporary, as if someone had staged it for a listing and forgotten to remove the occupant.

I crossed to the rig.

The headset came down over my eyes. The room vanished by degrees: desk, couch, laundry, black television screen. The haptic cuffs locked around my wrists and ankles with soft magnetic clicks. Motion gloves sealed along my fingers. The neural band settled against my temples, cool at first, then warm as the calibration lights blinked green.

My breathing slowed.

Not calm, exactly.

Focused.

There was a difference.

The login field appeared in the dark.

AETHERFALL: GENESIS

Below it, the guild notification pulsed.

404: AIM NOT FOUND
SCRIM LOBBY OPEN
OPPONENT: DEAD HAND

I flexed my fingers. The gloves caught the motion perfectly.

For the first time all night, the noise in my head arranged itself into something useful.

“Alright,” I said, and confirmed login. “Let’s go find out what breaks.”
