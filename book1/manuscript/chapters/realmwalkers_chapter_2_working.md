# Chapter Two
## The Facility

Marcus had been awake long enough for the apartment to stop feeling like his.

His suitcase stood beside the front door, packed sometime after midnight by a version of him who had apparently believed folding shirts still mattered. His backpack rested against it. Wallet. Keys. Chargers. Medication. Laptop. Two changes of clothes and nothing that answered the larger question of what someone packed for experimental brain surgery.

The apartment looked exactly the same.

Coffee cooling beside the sink. Shoes under the edge of the couch. A delivery box waiting to be broken down because apparently recycling remained part of the social contract until someone formally announced otherwise.

At 5:12 a.m., someone knocked.

Three firm taps.

Marcus stared at the door.

The knock did not make what had happened last night more real. It removed the possibility that reality might reconsider.

He crossed the living room and checked the camera beside the frame.

A man and a woman stood in the hall.

The man faced the door squarely. Dark utility jacket, plain shirt, practical boots. Close-cut hair. Nothing visibly armed, though his clothes had been selected to conceal information rather than provide it.

The woman beside him wore jeans, white sneakers, and a green jacket over a gray shirt. She looked like someone waiting for an early flight.

Marcus opened the door but left the chain engaged.

“Marcus Vye?” the man asked.

“That depends on why you’re asking.”

The woman almost smiled.

The man removed a credential wallet and held it where Marcus could see without leaning through the gap.

“Eldrin Shaw. This is Selia Moreno. We’re with a private security contractor retained by Astria. We’re here to escort you to the airport.”

Marcus studied the identification.

The credentials named the company, displayed photographs that matched the people in front of him, and contained enough holographic security features to suggest someone had worried about counterfeiting them.

That did not mean much. Astria employed people who could counterfeit reality.

“Private security,” Marcus said.

“Yes.”

“Not transportation.”

“Transportation is included.”

Marcus looked at him.

Eldrin’s expression did not change.

“Good distinction,” Marcus said.

Selia shifted half a step closer without crowding the door. “Astria assigned an escort because of the program and what happened last night. You can verify our credentials before you let us inside.”

Marcus already had his phone out.

The number connected to an Astria operator who knew his employee identification, the names of both escorts, his commercial flight number, and the minute they were expected to leave his apartment.

By the time the call ended, Marcus had acquired several new concerns. None improved his options.

He removed the chain.

“Come in.”

Eldrin entered first.

He stopped just inside and took in the living room, kitchen, balcony door, and short hall leading toward the bedroom. One sweep. No attempt to pretend he was admiring the space.

“Does anyone else have access to the apartment?” he asked.

“My landlord.”

“Anyone expected today?”

“No.”

“Alarm system?”

“Disarmed.”

Eldrin nodded once, apparently satisfied with a process Marcus had not known they were completing.

Selia followed him inside and glanced at the suitcase.

“Looks like you’re ready.”

“The luggage is.”

Her smile softened. “That counts for something.”

Eldrin checked his watch. “We need to leave in twelve minutes.”

Marcus looked toward the untouched coffee on the counter. “Generous.”

“It was fifteen before verification.”

Selia walked past him toward the living room. “That was almost a joke.”

“No,” Eldrin said.

Marcus looked between them.

Selia lowered her voice. “He gets defensive.”

“I can hear you.”

“Good. Saves time.”

Eldrin remained near the entrance, positioned where he could see both the hall outside and most of the apartment. Selia sat on the edge of the armchair without settling into it.

Marcus gestured toward the room. “Make yourselves comfortable. Or continue securing the couch. I need a minute.”

Eldrin glanced toward the bedroom hall, then back to Marcus.

“Five.”

“Twelve became five quickly.”

“Seven are for leaving the building.”

That answer sounded absurd until Marcus pictured the elevator, the parking lot, the suitcase, and whatever route Eldrin had already calculated through all three.

“Five,” Marcus agreed.

He stepped into his bedroom and left the door partly open.

The blinds were still closed. His monitor cast a dull rectangle across the desk. Aetherfall was no longer running, but a pale remnant of the interface remained burned into his vision whenever he looked at the dark screen.

The missing logout control.

The storm swallowing the arena.

Roth’s voice continuing as though none of it changed the meaning of consent.

Marcus sat on the edge of the bed.

Nothing moved in the apartment beyond the low hum of the air-conditioning. Eldrin and Selia did not whisper. No drawers opened. No one searched through his things.

Two strangers were sitting in his living room before sunrise, waiting to take him across the country so Astria could cut open his skull.

His phone was already in his hand.

His thumb found Discord before he made the decision to open it.

The thread with Serra remained where he had left it. Read. Unanswered, although neither of them had technically asked a question.

The messages were ten days old, from the morning after the Finalist Reception.

**Serra:** I checked. You really did qualify twenty-fifth.

**Marcus:** We prefer “the final team deemed worthy.”

**Serra:** You qualified by four points.

**Marcus:** Four points is a dominant margin if you stop comparing it to larger numbers.

**Serra:** You were more convincing last night.

**Marcus:** Different venue.

**Serra:** Better venue?

**Marcus:** The view was better.

**Serra:** It was.

He had read the exchange enough times to know it by shape.

She had brought the conversation back to the reception, then stopped just short of saying why it mattered. He had done the same.

Ten days later, he still could not tell whether that had been caution or an invitation.

Yesterday, Marcus had torn away Dead Hand’s hood and found Serra underneath it.

Seb had called him off. Mathias had warned him that everyone was watching.

Marcus had stayed anyway.

A knock touched the bedroom door.

“Two minutes,” Eldrin said.

Marcus locked the phone.

“You’re stealing time now.”

“You used three minutes and fourteen seconds.”

“That feels unnecessarily documented.”

“It wasn’t difficult.”

Marcus stood, checked his pockets, then checked them again because the first attempt had apparently lacked authority.

Wallet. Keys. Phone.

He carried his backpack into the living room.

Selia rose. “Everything?”

“I have all my chargers.”

“That’s usually the important part.”

Eldrin took the suitcase.

Marcus held on to the handle for half a second.

Eldrin waited rather than pulling.

“The bag stays with us until airline check-in,” he said. “It will be tagged through to California. At the facility, campus security will deliver it to the residential wing.”

Marcus released it.

“Do you explain every bag this thoroughly?”

“Only when someone doesn’t want to let go.”

Selia looked at Eldrin.

He opened the apartment door.

Marcus took one last look through the room.

Coffee beside the sink. Blank television. The shallow depression in the couch where he had spent most of the night failing to sleep.

He turned off the light and followed them out.

The lock clicked behind him.

---

The drive to the airport lasted twenty-three minutes.

Selia sat in the rear seat beside Marcus. Eldrin drove.

Charlotte passed beyond the glass in ordinary pieces. Delivery trucks. Traffic lights changing for empty intersections. A woman being pulled down the sidewalk by a dog that appeared to consider mornings a personal insult.

The world had not received the update.

“Did you eat?” Selia asked.

“No.”

She reached into her bag and held out a wrapped breakfast bar.

Marcus accepted it. “Astria issues emergency granola?”

“I do.”

“Why?”

“Because people who aren’t hungry at five become hungry at six.”

“That sounds uncomfortably evidence-based.”

“It keeps happening.”

From the front, Eldrin said, “Seven minutes.”

Marcus looked at the bar.

“Until hunger?”

“Airport.”

Selia glanced at Marcus. “He’s very destination-oriented.”

“I’d started to suspect.”

They reached the terminal before the conversation had anywhere else to go.

Astria had booked first-class seats on a commercial flight west. No private runway. No dark jet waiting beyond the hangars. Just check-in kiosks, rolling luggage, coffee lines, and a child repeatedly asking whether the plane could fall out of the sky.

Marcus found the noise reassuring.

Secret human experimentation seemed less likely beside a smoothie stand.

Not impossible. Just poorly branded.

Eldrin handled the checked luggage. Selia guided Marcus through security while remaining far enough away that he could plausibly believe he was moving under his own power.

The aircraft had two seats on each side of the aisle.

Marcus took the window. Selia sat beside him. Eldrin occupied the aisle seat across from them, positioned where he could see Marcus, the forward cabin, and anyone approaching from behind.

After takeoff, Marcus opened the airline magazine from the seat pocket.

The first article reviewed a mountain resort where employees carried guests’ skis.

The second concerned watches expensive enough to require their own security arrangements.

The third offered seven techniques for remaining productive on vacation, which seemed hostile to the underlying concept.

He reached the final page without retaining a sentence.

Selia glanced at the magazine. “Anything good?”

“There’s a hotel where someone carries your skis.”

“Do you ski?”

“No.”

“Then you’d have almost nothing to do.”

“That is the strongest endorsement so far.”

Across the aisle, Eldrin turned a page in his own magazine.

Marcus looked at him. “What did you get?”

“Baseball preview.”

Selia leaned slightly toward Marcus. “He has interests.”

“I had assumed. I was starting with tax policy.”

Eldrin did not look up. “The Braves need another starting pitcher.”

Marcus waited.

“That’s it?”

“That was the relevant part.”

Selia smiled.

Marcus returned the magazine to the seat pocket.

The cabin lights dimmed.

Selia closed her eyes. Eldrin continued reading. Marcus watched the clouds erase the ground beneath them.

He did not sleep.

---

A second vehicle collected them from the California airport.

Eldrin drove again. Selia sat in front, leaving Marcus alone in the rear seat with his reflection following him across the window.

The city thinned into broad roads and low hills.

The facility appeared first as fencing.

Not razor wire or concrete barriers. Dark metal set back behind landscaped ground, cameras mounted at measured intervals, and a gatehouse built from the same pale stone as the buildings beyond it.

Security with no need to advertise fear.

Eldrin lowered the window and presented their credentials. The guard compared all three faces to a tablet before raising the barrier.

The campus spread across several hills in low, pale buildings connected by covered walkways. Solar panels followed one slope. Young trees softened the lines of the architecture without blocking any camera for more than a second.

People in scrubs crossed between buildings carrying coffee.

The ordinary movement bothered Marcus more than armed guards would have.

At the main entrance, a campus security officer removed Marcus’s suitcase from the trunk and attached a numbered tag to the handle.

“It goes directly to your room,” Eldrin said.

Marcus watched the officer place it on a cart. “How long am I staying?”

“Overnight,” Selia said. “Testing begins this afternoon. Surgery is scheduled for tomorrow morning if you consent and receive medical clearance.”

“If.”

“Yes.”

She did not try to place more freedom inside the word than it could hold.

The lobby contained too much natural light and nowhere comfortable to stand. A wall of living plants rose behind the reception desk around an Astria logo in brushed steel.

A woman in a navy blazer approached before Marcus reached the desk.

“Mr. Vye?”

“Yes.”

“I’m Rebecca Vail. I coordinate participant intake for the neural-interface program.”

They shook hands.

“Dr. Mercer and Mr. Roth will meet with you after the other participants arrive. I’ll take you upstairs.”

Eldrin and Selia stopped at the elevators.

“This is where we hand you over,” Selia said.

Marcus glanced between them. “That sounds ominous.”

“We’ll remain on campus,” Eldrin said. “Internal security has responsibility from this point forward.”

“Should I be offended that I’ve become someone else’s responsibility?”

“No.”

Marcus looked at him.

Eldrin extended a hand.

Marcus took it.

“Good luck,” Eldrin said.

No dry qualification followed.

Marcus nodded. “Thanks.”

Selia touched his forearm briefly.

“Eat before the testing starts.”

“You’ve developed a theme.”

“You keep making it relevant.”

The elevator doors closed between them.

Ms. Vail selected the fourth floor.

“They’re good at their jobs,” she said.

Marcus watched the numbers change above the door. “I’m still deciding what their job was.”

“Getting you here safely.”

“We appear to have different definitions of the dangerous part.”

Ms. Vail did not answer.

The doors opened.

She led him through a quiet corridor to a conference room overlooking the western side of the campus. A long table occupied most of the space. Water carafes sat at both ends. Eight chairs, legal pads, pens placed parallel to the table’s edge.

A large manila folder waited at the nearest seat.

Marcus’s name was printed across the front.

Ms. Vail indicated it. “You may read through the preliminary materials. I’ll return when the others arrive.”

“How long?”

“Mr. Rainier’s vehicle just cleared the gate.”

Marcus looked toward the window.

“That specific?”

“This is a secured medical campus.”

“That explains how. Not whether it’s comforting.”

A small smile touched her mouth.

“Coffee will be here shortly.”

She left him alone.

Marcus sat and opened the folder.

The title page read:

**ASTRIA NEURAL-INTERFACE PROGRAM**  
**PARTICIPANT DISCLOSURE AND INFORMED CONSENT**

The first section explained that participation was voluntary.

The second explained that the procedure could result in death.

Marcus was still deciding whether the order had been intentional when the door opened.

Seb entered.

Dark sweater. Jeans. Visitor badge clipped neatly at his waist. He looked tired but put together, the way Seb usually did when everyone around him needed him not to look tired.

Marcus stood.

Seb crossed the room and pulled him into a hug.

Not their usual public shoulder-clasp. A full embrace, held one beat longer than either of them would normally allow.

When they separated, Seb kept one hand on Marcus’s shoulder.

“You all right?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Marcus looked at him.

Seb’s mouth twitched. “I didn’t want to be the only one.”

There he was.

Not all the way.

Enough.

Marcus nodded toward the folder. “Death is on page two.”

Seb sat beside him and opened his own packet. “What’s on page one?”

“Voluntary participation.”

“Strong opening.”

“How was the flight?”

“Long.”

Seb looked down at the document.

The answer closed around something Marcus was not going to force open in an empty conference room.

Micah had been dead before the scrim. Seb had logged in anyway. Led anyway. Kept the team moving until Astria took the match away from them.

Seb rested both hands on the folder.

For several seconds, nothing in him moved.

Then the door opened again, and warmth returned to his face before Brent and Mathias entered.

“There they are,” Seb said.

Brent pointed at Marcus. “You look terrible.”

“Seb already used that.”

“Did he say it correctly?”

“He meant it supportively.”

“That’s less useful.”

Mathias reached Marcus first and hugged him before Marcus had fully stood.

“Good to see you.”

“You too.”

Mathias released him and studied his face.

“No, genuinely.”

“I believed you.”

“You hesitated.”

“I was moved.”

“Internally?”

“Deeply.”

Brent offered Marcus his hand, then used it to pull him into a one-armed embrace.

They had all met before. Tournaments. Conventions. A beach trip that had produced four straight days of rain. The weekend Mathias had persuaded them to cross two state lines for a restaurant because he had seen the owner interviewed in a documentary.

The restaurant had been excellent.

Brent still maintained this did not justify the drive.

For several minutes, the room found an older rhythm.

Flights. Pickup times. Brent’s review of airport coffee. Mathias’s conversation with a retired teacher who had spent forty years photographing covered bridges.

Seb let the conversation move without directing it. He laughed when something was funny, asked whether Brent had eaten, and told Mathias he wanted to see the bridge photographs later.

Only once, when the others were speaking, did Marcus see the expression leave Seb’s face.

It returned before anyone turned toward him.

The conference-room door opened.

Serra came in first, tall and athletic in dark jeans, a rust-colored henley, and an olive overshirt worn open. Her hair was impossible to miss—fiery copper-red, straight and smooth, falling in a heavy, voluminous sheet to her lower ribs. Auburn depth and finer golden strands moved through the copper when she crossed beneath the lights.

Mara followed half a step behind her, smaller and slim, in a charcoal cardigan over a pale gray shirt, dark jeans, and worn sneakers—comfortable clothes that asked for little attention. Her dirty-blonde hair fell between her shoulder blades and mid-back, framing softer features and hazel-green eyes that moved across the people, folders, exits, and arrangement of chairs before settling anywhere directly.

Serra took in the four of them and smiled.

“So this is 404 without health bars.”

Brent leaned back in his chair. “Disappointed?”

“A little.”

“You’ll recover.”

“I usually do.”

She crossed the room and greeted Seb first, then Brent and Mathias, moving easily through the introductions as though they had gathered under circumstances merely inconvenient instead of impossible.

Most of them had met Mara briefly at the Finalist Reception, though Marcus doubted any of the men had exchanged more than a few sentences with her.

Serra drew her farther into the room.

“And this is Mara. If she notices something about you, she’s probably right.”

Mara gave her a look.

Serra smiled. “She hates when I tell people that.”

“I hate when you tell them before I’ve decided to notice anything.”

Brent looked down at himself. “Do we get a warning when she starts?”

“No,” Mara said.

“Good system.”

The edge of her mouth moved.

She greeted Seb and Brent, then stopped beside Mathias.

“You were the one who found the northern route.”

Mathias looked surprised. “Eventually.”

“You checked the ice instead of the walls.”

“The walls looked designed to be noticed.”

Mara’s attention sharpened.

“They were.”

Then Serra looked toward Marcus.

“Twenty-five.”

“Three.”

“You remembered.”

“There were fewer numbers involved than you’d expect.”

“That probably helped.”

“Dramatically.”

Seb gestured toward the chairs. “Sit down before rankings become the official topic.”

“Marcus started it,” Serra said.

“I was identified by number.”

“You knew what it meant.”

“I had context.”

Mara sat beside Serra. Mathias chose the chair across from them. Brent settled near the end of the table.

The room now contained four members of 404 and the two Dead Hand players who had helped dismantle their formation the night before.

For practice.

Then the storm had swallowed the arena, the logout control had failed, and the match had stopped belonging to any of them.

The silence that followed was brief but complete.

Dr. Mercer entered carrying two cardboard trays of coffee.

He was in his mid-forties, with dark hair beginning to gray at the temples and the posture of someone accustomed to spending hours on his feet. No white coat. Blue shirt, sleeves rolled to his forearms. His identification badge read **Adrian Mercer, MD, PhD**.

He placed the trays in the center of the table.

“Before anyone says anything, yes, this is an obvious attempt to make the room feel less clinical.”

Brent reached for a cup. “Does admitting it improve the coffee?”

“No.”

“Then I respect the honesty.”

Mercer passed the cups around.

“I’m Adrian Mercer. I’m a functional neurosurgeon, and I direct Astria’s neural-interface program. If you choose to proceed and receive medical clearance today, I’ll lead the surgical team tomorrow morning.”

Marcus accepted a cup. “You’re operating on all six of us?”

“I’ll supervise all six procedures. Three surgical teams will work in sequence.”

“That sounds more responsible.”

“It is.”

Mercer sat near the head of the table.

“Today consists of baseline testing. Bloodwork. Imaging. Neurocognitive and psychiatric assessment. Motor and sensory mapping. We’ll repeat anything that produces an abnormal result and determine the surgical order tonight.”

“Assuming we sign,” Brent said.

“Assuming you sign,” Mercer agreed.

Mathias glanced at the wall clock. “Was tomorrow always the plan?”

Mercer’s gaze moved across the room.

“The timing changed last night.”

Before anyone could ask why, the door opened behind Marcus.

“Then we should begin there.”

Marcus knew the voice before he turned.

The room changed.

The man from the scrim entered wearing a dark gray suit and carrying a narrow leather portfolio beneath one arm.

Without the storm and the dead interface surrounding him, Roth looked almost ordinary. Late fifties. Trim. Silver beginning at the sides of his hair.

The voice was the same.

Measured. Controlled. The voice that had explained their choices while removing one of them.

Brent set down his coffee.

The warmth in Serra’s expression withdrew somewhere inaccessible.

Roth closed the door and took the empty chair beside Mercer.

“Daniel Roth. General counsel for Astria.”

“You were there,” Mathias said.

“Yes.”

“You took over the match,” Brent said.

“Astria activated the session’s emergency-control protocols and assumed command.”

“You disabled logout.”

“Yes.”

The answer landed harder for its lack of defense.

Ms. Vail entered behind him carrying five additional folders. She placed one in front of each participant, then took a seat near the wall with a tablet.

Roth opened his portfolio.

“The verbal assent recorded last night authorized your transport to this facility and today’s noninvasive evaluations. It did not authorize surgery. No invasive procedure will occur unless each of you signs the consent documents in front of you.”

Brent rested a hand on his folder.

“Consent.”

“It is the legal term,” Roth said. “I understand why you object to it.”

“You do?”

“You made the decision after Astria prevented you from leaving the session. I am not asking you to treat those circumstances as neutral.”

No one answered.

Roth continued.

“You are free to leave this facility. You will not be detained. Astria will arrange and fund your return travel.”

“And then?” Seb asked.

“Then you return home.”

“That simple?”

“No,” Roth said. “But you are not being held here.”

Seb nodded once.

Not agreement. A marked distinction.

Marcus looked toward Mercer. “You said the timing changed.”

Mercer touched the tablet in front of him.

The wall display activated.

Six bright lines appeared against a field of thinner gray traces, each moving in uneven waves across the screen.

“These are neural-feedback telemetry streams from last night’s scrimmage,” Mercer said. “Aetherfall records them during full immersion to monitor cognitive load, sensory stress, motor intent, autonomic response, and neurological safety. The gray traces belong to the other players in the session. The six highlighted records are yours.”

Serra leaned forward slightly. “Why are ours separated?”

“Because yours began doing something the others did not.”

Mercer advanced the recording to the first vertical marker. At the beginning of the match, the six traces moved independently. Near the marker, a faint shape began repeating between them—not synchronization, exactly, but an ordered response passing from one line into another.

“This is where we first detected the anomaly,” Mercer said.

Marcus studied the time beneath the marker. “Before you took over.”

“Yes.”

Brent looked toward Roth. “Then why were you watching?”

“Astria had already identified a broader candidate cohort among players in the championship field,” Roth said. “Everyone in that match was being evaluated through data Astria was legally authorized to review.”

“All of us?” Serra asked.

“Every player in the session belonged to the broader cohort. You six were not the only candidates.”

“And none of us knew,” Brent said.

“Astria’s agreement with the league permits neurological safety review and anonymized research use of telemetry generated through its immersion systems.”

“Anonymized until you sent people to our homes.”

“Yes.”

Roth said it without pretending the transition was insignificant.

Mercer advanced the recording to the next marker. The repeating structure became more pronounced, the highlighted traces drawing closer without losing their separate shapes.

“This is where Ms. Hawthorne and Mr. Donovan entered sustained close-range engagement.”

Brent studied the graph. “You mean where she nearly killed me.”

“In the game,” Serra said.

“Still reviewing the distinction.”

Mercer advanced again.

“This is where Mr. Vye committed to the opening created by that engagement.”

The shared pattern strengthened.

The next marker produced a sharper change. Marcus’s and Serra’s traces moved first, the repeating structure suddenly pronounced between them. The other four followed a fraction later.

Mercer paused the recording.

“This is where Mr. Vye and Ms. Hawthorne recognized one another.”

Marcus looked at Serra.

She was still watching the graph.

Brent’s attention shifted between them, but Mercer continued before the room could turn the marker into another subject.

At the next point, Mathias’s and Mara’s traces developed their own sustained exchange. Each response from one was answered by the other, and the larger six-person pattern tightened again.

“This is where Mr. Sterling and Ms. Valeria became one another’s primary interaction.”

Mara leaned closer to the display. “You’re treating interaction as the cause.”

“I’m treating it as the strongest correlation visible in the data.”

“Those are different.”

“Yes.”

Mathias pointed toward the gray traces. “Everyone else was interacting.”

“They were.”

“And none of them produced this?”

“No.”

Mercer zoomed the display outward. The six highlighted traces receded into layer after layer of recorded activity until the background resembled static.

“Astria retains neurological safety telemetry from every player who has ever used one of its immersion chairs,” he said. “Millions of recorded sessions. Competitive matches, casual play, raids, social spaces, mass events. We have never observed this pattern.”

He restored the six traces.

“Not once.”

Brent rubbed his jaw. “So six people happened to be in the same game, fought each other in ordinary ways, and your equipment decided to invent a new language.”

“The system did not invent the signal,” Mercer said. “It recorded something we do not know how to interpret.”

Marcus looked toward the upper band of the display. Several values had turned amber before the recognition marker and climbed toward red afterward.

“What are those?”

Mercer expanded the measurements.

“Neurological load. The convergence was not merely structural. As it strengthened, it increased cortical excitation, sensory-feedback demand, and autonomic stress across all six of you.”

The recognition marker remained the largest jump.

Mercer advanced the recording toward Astria’s intervention. Three warning indicators crossed into red. The other three continued climbing.

“By this point, three of you had crossed the system’s seizure-risk threshold,” Mercer said. “The remaining three were approaching it. Had the trend continued, seizure or loss of consciousness was the immediate concern. We do not know what would have followed.”

Brent looked toward Roth. “So you locked us inside for our health.”

“The emergency override was medically justified,” Mercer said. “I can only speak to that portion of what followed.”

Roth did not object to the distinction.

Mercer advanced the graph past the moment Astria assumed control. The traces remained convergent, but their neurological-load indicators stopped rising and began to stabilize.

“We changed the sensory environment and prevented an uncontrolled disconnect while the safety system reduced the feedback load,” Mercer said. “Abrupt termination during the escalation could have worsened the response.”

“You kept us in after the numbers came down,” Serra said.

Roth met her gaze. “Yes.”

“Why?”

“The six primary traces were present in one controlled instance. The anomaly was already weakening. Astria had an opportunity to identify the participants, confirm the data, and present the existing program.”

“You used a medical emergency as a recruitment window,” Mathias said.

“Yes.”

The word settled over the room without softening.

Seb leaned forward. “Did you arrange the scrim?”

“No,” Roth said. “Astria had no role in scheduling it.”

“Then you just happened to have all six of us together.”

“Yes.”

“And you were ready to make an offer.”

“The candidate pool and demonstration system already existed.”

Marcus looked toward Mercer. “What exactly did you show us?”

Mercer changed the display. Six blurred panels replaced the graph.

“Each demonstration was generated from long-term performance telemetry, cognitive-response modeling, neurological data, and behavioral profiling. They were individualized projections of functions the implant may be able to support.”

“May,” Seb said.

“Yes.”

“What we saw wasn’t real.”

“The environment was simulated. The underlying functions were not invented.”

Roth folded his hands. “Astria selected a demonstration likely to communicate the implant’s value in terms relevant to each participant.”

Brent gave a short laugh. “You found the pressure point.”

“Yes.”

Again, no defense.

Mara looked from the panels to Roth. “And all of this came from data you were permitted to collect?”

“The safety telemetry was collected under Astria’s platform and league agreements,” Roth said. “The behavioral profiles were assembled from information Astria already possessed or was licensed to use. The individualized selection of psychological leverage was not disclosed to you.”

“That sounds more honest when you call it leverage,” Mathias said.

“It is more precise.”

Marcus looked at Mercer. “Can the implant actually do what we saw?”

“We believe it can support the underlying capacities,” Mercer said. “That is not the same as guaranteeing the exact outcome.”

“For example?”

Mercer glanced toward Marcus’s blurred panel.

“Expanded sensory integration. Accelerated pattern recognition. Predictive modeling. Direct cognitive access to information normally divided across multiple systems. The implant may allow you to integrate behavioral, sensory, and contextual information quickly enough to make better predictive judgments. It cannot guarantee those judgments will be correct. It cannot guarantee you will act on them well.”

The demonstration had known exactly where to cut.

Not because it promised omniscience.

Because it promised the missing second between understanding and failure.

Serra kept her attention on her own panel. “You showed each of us the most favorable expression.”

“A favorable model,” Mercer said.

“The one most likely to make us agree.”

“Yes.”

Mercer answered with less ease than Roth.

Brent opened his folder. “And now the favorable model meets the page-two death warning.”

“That is why we’re here,” Mercer said.

He dismissed the display.

“The telemetry does not show permanent injury. Your readings returned to baseline overnight. It does show that the six of you, together, produced an unprecedented response capable of pushing your nervous systems toward an unsafe threshold.”

“Will it happen again?” Seb asked.

“We do not know.”

“Does the implant prevent it?”

“We do not know that either. The implant was not built as a treatment for this anomaly.”

“Then why accelerate the surgery?” Mara asked.

“Because the response changed our assessment of your compatibility with the system,” Mercer said. “And because the pattern was transient. If the implant can detect the same phenomenon at greater resolution, waiting may mean losing our best opportunity to understand it.”

“That benefits Astria,” Brent said.

“Yes.”

“Does it benefit us?”

Mercer looked around the table. “That depends on whether you want what the implant may provide.”

Roth opened the first folder.

He moved through the medical and legal terms without trying to make them sound smaller.

The procedure required placement of a neural lattice and processor assembly adjacent to regions involved in sensory integration, motor processing, and memory consolidation.

Known risks included infection, hemorrhage, stroke, seizure, impaired movement, language disruption, memory loss, chronic neuropathic pain, altered emotional regulation, coma, and death.

Unknown risks included hallucinations, cross-sensory perception, dissociation, and persistent changes in how the brain organized incoming information.

The complete implant had not been used in its current configuration on a human subject.

Mara stopped Mercer during the animal-trial section.

“How many failed?”

Mercer looked at her.

“Enough that I won’t call the procedure safe.”

“How many?”

He gave her the number.

The room became quieter after that.

Astria would fund transportation, surgery, housing, rehabilitation, and long-term medical monitoring. Compensation would be paid whether the procedure succeeded or failed. Separate schedules established permanent-disability and death benefits.

Astria would retain access to implant telemetry and research data generated through the program. Personally identifying information would be restricted. The underlying data would remain Astria property.

The program was confidential. Participants could consult independent attorneys, but those attorneys would need to sign nondisclosure agreements before reviewing protected technical material.

The implant could not be removed without further surgery.

Removal might not reverse neurological changes caused by the device.

Roth did not rush.

Mercer interrupted twice to replace legal phrasing with medical language. Roth accepted both corrections without resistance.

When they reached the final page, Ms. Vail distributed pens.

Roth closed his portfolio.

“Dr. Mercer and I will leave the room. Ms. Vail can arrange secure calls with independent counsel for anyone who requests one. Take the time you need.”

Brent glanced toward the clock. “Testing starts after lunch.”

“If you choose to proceed.”

“And if we take six hours?”

“Testing begins later.”

Roth stood.

Mercer remained seated.

Marcus studied him.

“If those were your readings, would you do it?”

Mercer looked toward the blank wall where the telemetry had been.

“Yes.”

The answer came quickly enough to change the room.

“Because you trust the implant?” Marcus asked.

“No.”

Mercer folded his hands.

“Because I would need to know what the machine had seen.”

He stood and followed Roth out.

Ms. Vail left last, closing the door behind her.

For several seconds, no one spoke.

Brent rotated his folder on the table.

Mathias watched him. “Expecting it to improve from another direction?”

“It hasn’t yet.”

Serra leaned back in her chair. “So nobody knows why the six of us did that.”

“Nobody in this building,” Mara said.

Marcus looked toward her.

She tapped the closed folder.

“They showed us the result. Not the assumptions underneath it.”

Brent frowned. “Meaning?”

“They were watching a larger group. They didn’t arrange the scrim. They didn’t expect the pattern. Then it happened and they changed the plan.”

Mathias nodded slowly. “They didn’t find what they were looking for.”

“They found something they decided was better,” Mara said.

Seb folded his arms on the table.

“Anyone want counsel?”

No one answered.

“Anyone seriously considering walking?”

Brent raised one hand.

Mathias did the same.

After a moment, Marcus did too.

Serra looked around the table. “I’m considering it. I’m not leaving.”

“Why?” Seb asked.

She looked down at her folder.

“Because I know they picked the demonstration to make me want it.”

Her fingers rested against the cover.

“It worked.”

No embarrassment. No excuse.

Brent leaned back. “That may be the least reassuring argument so far.”

“It wasn’t an argument.”

Mara looked toward Serra, then at her own folder.

“They know what each of us wants,” she said. “They don’t know why the six of us fit together.”

“Neither do we,” Mathias said.

“No.”

Seb turned toward Marcus. “Where are you?”

Marcus opened the folder to the signature page.

The first yes had happened inside Aetherfall with the exit removed.

This one came with coffee, risk tables, and a pen.

Cleaner presentation. Same underlying shape.

“I don’t trust them,” Marcus said.

“No argument,” Brent said.

“I believe they saw something.”

Seb nodded.

“And I want what they showed me.”

The admission felt worse aloud.

Brent looked at him for a moment, then down at his own folder.

“Yeah.”

Not agreement with Marcus’s reasoning.

Recognition of his own.

Marcus looked at the signature line.

“Knowing they chose the demonstration because I’d want it doesn’t make me want it less.”

Mathias exhaled quietly. “That’s the problem.”

Seb picked up his pen.

He did not make a speech. He read the final page once more, then signed.

No request that anyone follow.

Brent watched him.

“Still a gun,” he said.

Seb looked at the signature.

“I know.”

Brent signed next.

Mathias reread one paragraph, then added his name.

Mara returned to the data-rights section, marked a line with her finger, and remained there long enough that Serra glanced toward her.

Mara turned back and signed.

Serra signed.

Marcus was last.

Not because he had reached a different conclusion.

Because some part of him wanted the delay recorded somewhere, even if only inside his own memory.

He placed the pen against the page.

The signature looked the same as it did on expense reports, employment forms, and birthday cards.

Nothing in it showed the dead logout control.

Nothing showed the storm.

Nothing showed what Astria had offered him inside it.

Nothing showed how many versions of no they had removed before handing him the pen.

Marcus signed.

Seb opened the door and called for Ms. Vail.

She returned with Roth behind her.

Roth checked the final page of each folder.

“We have all six signatures,” he said. “Testing begins after lunch.”

No one answered.
