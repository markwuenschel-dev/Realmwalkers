# Chapter Three
## The Passage

The first test involved a reflex hammer, a wall of lights, and a neurologist who wanted Marcus to count backward by sevens while following her finger with his eyes.

He did what she asked.

The room had been divided into two stations. Marcus occupied one. Mathias sat at the other with adhesive sensors at his temples while a technician explained a sequence of tones, images, and timed responses.

“What exactly changes between the first and second pass?” Mathias asked.

“The stimuli remain the same,” the technician said. “We measure adaptation, attention drift, and response inhibition.”

Mathias looked at the display. “So improvement and anticipation can produce the same result.”

“Sometimes.”

“How do you separate them?”

The technician paused, then turned the monitor enough to show him a second set of traces.

Marcus returned his attention to the neurologist before she could tell him to.

That was Mathias. Put him in an unfamiliar country, an abandoned building, or an experimental medical program and he would start looking for the route by which it had become itself. He did not need the answer immediately. He needed to know which part of the answer was still moving.

Marcus understood the impulse. He was less interested in talking through it today.

A light flashed in the upper-right corner of his screen. He pressed the corresponding button.

Another appeared low and left. Then center. Then two in quick succession.

The machine recorded the delay between stimulus and response in fractions of a millisecond. Each answer became another point on a graph no one had offered to show him.

The rest of the morning followed the same principle. Grip strength. Balance. Hearing. Proprioception. Eye tracking. Memory. A series of faces displayed for less than a second, followed by questions about expression, familiarity, and intent. A card showed one shape, then another that was either identical or almost identical, and Marcus answered before the next card replaced it.

During the third sequence, he missed the same light twice.

The technician restarted the test.

Marcus missed it again.

“Was that one offset?” he asked.

“No.”

“It entered from a different angle.”

“The presentation was identical.”

Marcus looked at the screen. The software had already reduced the moment to a red mark in a column of green ones.

“Can I see the playback?”

The technician hesitated.

Not refusal. Procedure searching for permission.

She opened the recording.

The light had not entered from a different angle. It had not entered at all. The program had displayed it fully formed, exactly where she said it had.

Marcus watched the clip a second time.

His perception had supplied motion the screen did not contain.

“Again,” he said.

The technician reset the sequence.

This time he caught it.

She entered a note into the file.

“What did you write?”

“That you requested an additional pass.”

“That is accurate and strategically incomplete.”

“It is a medical record, Mr. Vye.”

“That has not historically prevented interpretation.”

She almost smiled. Almost.

The testing continued.

They drew enough blood to make the labeled tubes seem aspirational. Electrodes went onto his scalp, his neck, and the inside of his wrists. A psychiatrist asked about medication, concussions, migraines, panic attacks, sleep paralysis, hallucinations, dissociation, and whether Marcus had ever experienced a part of his body as foreign to him.

He answered plainly.

That seemed to encourage follow-up questions.

By the time they moved him into imaging, the shape of Astria’s interest had become clearer without becoming more comforting. The tests were not built only to establish whether he could survive the procedure. They were building a map detailed enough to recognize what changed afterward.

The MRI reduced the universe to mechanical knocking and a white plastic ceiling six inches above his face.

A technician spoke through his headphones between scans.

“Remain still. The next sequence will measure activation under guided recall.”

Marcus closed his eyes.

“Picture somewhere safe.”

His apartment appeared first.

The image lasted less than a second before Eldrin and Selia stood inside it, waiting to take him away.

He replaced it with 404’s guild hall in Aetherfall.

That held until wind struck the windows and the logout control failed.

“Picture someone you trust.”

Seb.

The answer came quickly enough to leave no room for a cleaner one.

“Picture a recent event with strong emotional significance.”

A terrace beneath city lights.

Serra turning toward him.

Marcus opened his eyes.

The scanner remained close and white above him.

“Please stay still,” the technician said.

“I am still.”

“Your head moved.”

Marcus closed his eyes again and let the machine have the damaged version of the moment: the scrim, her hood coming loose beneath his hand, recognition arriving half a second before judgment.

That was also true.

It was simply not the first truth.

When the testing team finally released him, his skin smelled faintly of adhesive and hospital soap. A strip near his hairline pulled where an electrode had been removed too quickly.

Mathias emerged from the adjoining room carrying his coat and a packet of postoperative instructions.

“Anything interesting?” he asked.

“They confirmed I have knees.”

Mathias waited.

Marcus adjusted the collar of his shirt. “One visual test registered motion that wasn’t there.”

“Prediction?”

“Maybe. The system recorded it as an error.”

“That isn’t the same classification.”

“No.”

Mathias looked back toward the examination room. “Did they know the difference?”

“They knew enough to write it down.”

Mathias accepted that provisionally. “My spatial test kept changing what it called a route.”

“Did the map change?”

“No. The goal did.”

Marcus looked at him.

Mathias’s expression had the quiet satisfaction of someone who had found a door where the wall was supposed to be.

They entered the hallway together.

Serra and Mara waited across from the next examination room.

Mara sat in one of the molded chairs with a clipboard balanced on her knee. Her cardigan had been folded beside her, leaving the pale gray shirt beneath. She had reached the final page of a form dense enough that the margins appeared to be under pressure.

Serra stood near the wall. She had drawn her hair over one shoulder to keep it clear of the painted surface, one hand loosely holding the length near her waist.

She pushed away when Marcus and Mathias approached.

“You look disappointed,” she said.

“They promised experimental medicine,” Marcus replied. “So far they’ve mostly confirmed I arrived with the expected number of limbs.”

Mathias glanced at him. “They found something on the visual test.”

Serra’s attention sharpened. “What?”

“I saw motion the machine didn’t produce.”

Mara looked up from the form. “Once?”

“Three times. Same stimulus.”

“Did it change after you watched the playback?”

“I caught it on the next pass.”

Mara considered that, then looked down at the page again without pretending the answer had resolved anything.

A nurse-administrator emerged carrying two thin folders.

“Mr. Sterling? Ms. Valeria?”

Mathias raised a hand.

“We need clarification on several intake responses before we can finalize your imaging review. There’s a desk around the corner.”

Mathias accepted one of the folders. “Clarification sounds less serious than supplemental testing.”

“It is paperwork.”

“That is the best possible version.”

Mara stood and handed over her clipboard.

Before following the nurse, she looked once from Serra to Marcus, then toward Mathias.

He caught it.

Neither of them said anything until they disappeared around the corner.

Serra watched them go.

Then she turned back to Marcus.

“You really didn’t know.”

“At the reception?”

“You knew my first name.”

“It was the name you gave me.”

“You studied Dead Hand.”

“I studied the rogue.” Marcus folded the corner of his instruction packet, noticed he was doing it, and flattened it again. “Your profile involved a hood, an edited portrait, and lighting aggressive enough to qualify as misdirection.”

Serra’s mouth curved. “You don’t watch interviews?”

“I avoid content designed to make strangers feel familiar.”

“That sounds lonely.”

“It was mostly efficient.”

“Mostly?”

He looked at her.

She let the question remain where she had placed it.

“You were better than I expected,” Serra said.

“How much better?”

“Enough.”

“I’ll take it.”

Her eyes stayed on him.

“You stayed too long,” she said.

Marcus knew which moment she meant. Seb calling him back. Mathias warning him that everyone was watching. The arena narrowing until the match had become the distance between Serra and him.

“So did you.”

“I know.”

No joke followed.

Around the corner, a chair scraped against the floor. Mathias said something too low to make out. Mara answered.

When they came back into view, Mathias slowed.

“Again,” he said quietly.

Mara glanced at him. “You noticed that too?”

“I did.”

“Then stop looking pleased about it.”

“I’m not pleased.”

“You were about to be.”

By the time they reached the others, both had arranged their expressions into something neutral enough to deny the exchange.

Mathias held up his folder. “Apparently I travel too often for several of Astria’s boxes.”

“That sounds like a design failure,” Serra said.

“It is.”

The examination-room door opened.

“Ms. Hawthorne?”

Serra gathered her hair behind her and stepped toward the room.

As she passed Marcus, she said, “Try not to invent any new motion while I’m gone.”

“I’ll limit myself to documented reality.”

“That would be new.”

Then she disappeared inside.

Mara watched the door close.

Mathias looked at her.

This time she did not tell him to stop.

---

Testing ended shortly after seven.

Astria fed them in the residential wing, where six private rooms opened onto a common area that looked more like an executive hospitality suite than part of a medical facility.

A single oversized L-shaped sectional wrapped around a low square table. Charging surfaces had been built into the armrests. One wall held a recessed display and dark cabinets that opened without visible handles. Smart-glass windows overlooked the campus, dimmed now against the evening glare. Across from them, a compact kitchenette had been stocked with coffee, bottled water, snacks, and enough carefully labeled food to suggest Astria expected dietary preferences to become evidence.

A wooden rocker sat near the windows.

Brent claimed it before anyone else had finished looking around.

“This one moves.”

Seb pressed down on the sectional cushion. “So does this.”

“Not repeatedly.”

Dinner waited beneath metal covers along the kitchenette counter. Chicken, rice, roasted vegetables, salad. Six cards listed allergies, dietary restrictions, and the exact time each of them had to stop eating before surgery.

Brent read his.

“Nothing after midnight.”

“You have five hours,” Seb said.

“That is the kind of thinking that creates shortages.”

“You’re standing in front of enough food for twelve people.”

“Then someone planned correctly.”

They served themselves and settled according to the affiliations that required the least thought.

Serra and Mara took the short side of the sectional together. Seb sat near the far end of the long side, with Marcus beside him and Mathias beyond Marcus. Brent angled the rocker toward the table, technically separate from both groups and close enough to comment on either.

For a while, the two teams remained visible.

Conversation stayed with safe subjects. The tests. The facility. The psychiatric interviewer who had asked Marcus three versions of the same question and seemed disappointed when his answer remained consistent.

“They weren’t the same question,” Marcus said.

Brent looked over his plate. “How different?”

“One asked whether I’d seen something that wasn’t there. One asked whether I’d mistaken something real for something else. The third asked whether anyone else agreed with me.”

Mara looked up. “Those are different.”

“Thank you.”

“The third one is about corroboration, not perception.”

Marcus pointed his fork toward her. “Exactly.”

Brent shook his head. “You two found the one acceptable way to make that worse.”

Mara’s mouth moved around the beginning of a smile.

Seb asked her what Astria had done with her testing, and she gave him the short version: attention tracking, visual interference, pattern recognition. He asked what the interference looked like rather than whether she had passed. That got him a longer answer.

Across the table, Serra took one of the roasted vegetables from Mara’s plate.

Mara looked down at the empty space.

“You had those.”

“Mine were smaller.”

“They came from the same tray.”

“Then yours had better placement.”

Mara moved the rest farther away.

Mathias asked whether anyone had been told the procedure order.

“Tomorrow morning,” Serra said. “They’re finalizing it tonight.”

Brent glanced at the cards beside the food. “They know when we stop eating but not whose skull they open first.”

“Astria understands priorities,” Marcus said.

Seb smiled into his glass.

He asked Mathias how a conversation with one of the technicians had turned into a comparison of rail systems in Singapore and Zurich. When Brent reviewed the structural failings of airport coffee lids, Seb made him explain the design he would use instead. Serra took that design apart on aesthetic grounds, and by the time Brent defended himself, Mara had joined in.

The food disappeared. The dishwasher accepted the plates with a muted electronic tone. Then they returned to the sectional and discovered that having nothing in their hands made the room quieter.

Seb opened one of the cabinets beneath the display.

Board games filled the first shelf, most of them with enough pieces to create a second administrative burden.

He found a deck of cards in the drawer below.

“This?”

Serra held out her hand. “Bullshit?”

Brent leaned forward in the rocker. “Finally, honest branding.”

Seb tossed her the deck. “You dealing?”

“You found it.”

“I’m delegating.”

“You haven’t earned that yet.”

She shuffled anyway.

The rules took less than a minute. Cards went down facedown in ascending order. The player announced what they had supposedly placed. Anyone could challenge. The loser took the pile.

The first round began with the caution of six people who understood one another unevenly.

Seb placed two cards.

“Two threes.”

Brent watched him for a moment. “Bullshit.”

Seb turned them over.

Two threes.

Brent collected the pile.

Marcus looked at him. “You’re not reading the cards.”

“What am I reading?”

“The fact that he looks comfortable.”

Brent glanced at Seb, who remained comfortably unhelpful.

“That’s suspicious.”

“It’s Seb.”

“Exactly.”

Serra played one card.

“Four.”

Marcus studied the speed of her hand, the angle of the card, and the almost careless way she leaned back afterward.

“Bullshit.”

She turned over a four.

Marcus pulled the pile toward himself.

“You decided before I played,” Serra said.

“I updated after.”

“Not successfully.”

“I didn’t say it was a good update.”

By the time play circled back around, Mara had shed nearly half her hand without attracting a challenge.

Marcus noticed only when she placed a single card and Mathias hesitated over it.

“Seven,” Mara said.

Mathias let it pass.

Marcus looked at the cards remaining in her hand, then at everyone else.

“Interesting.”

Brent sighed. “What?”

“Nobody is challenging the only player who hasn’t volunteered a conclusion about anyone.”

Mara turned toward him. “You noticed after I was almost finished.”

“Yes.”

“That seems less useful.”

“It is.”

Serra looked pleased on Mara’s behalf.

The game moved faster after that.

Brent challenged any claim that seemed structurally excessive. Mathias sounded equally thoughtful whether he was lying or telling the truth, which made him appear dishonest far more often than he was. Seb occasionally encouraged someone to challenge him, which only made Brent more certain the cards were real.

Serra lied cleanly but not consistently. Marcus began to suspect she enjoyed controlling when he challenged more than she cared about winning the exchange.

She placed two cards.

“Queens.”

Her expression did not change.

That itself had become unhelpful.

Marcus looked at her fingers. No tension. No hurry. She had placed the cards slightly nearer to him than the center of the table.

An invitation, or an attempt to make him think it was one.

“Bullshit.”

Serra turned over two queens.

The room reacted before Marcus did.

Brent laughed. Mathias winced in sympathy. Mara glanced from the cards to Serra as though she had expected exactly this.

Marcus took the pile.

“You wanted me to call that,” he said.

“They were queens.”

“That wasn’t the claim.”

“That was literally the claim.”

“You presented truth with deceptive intent.”

Serra leaned back. “That sounds like a personal problem.”

“It’s a distinct category.”

“Only to you.”

Seb gathered the cards Marcus had failed to contain. “I’m ruling in favor of the person with the queens.”

“You have no authority here,” Marcus said.

“I found the deck.”

Serra smiled. “He’s earned it now.”

By the next round, everyone had shifted closer to the table.

Mathias had leaned across Marcus twice to see the pile before giving up and moving to the short leg of the sectional. Mara made room for him without interrupting her conversation with Serra. Serra slid nearer the bend to reach the cards. Marcus moved into the space beside her when Seb stood to refill the water pitcher, and Seb reclaimed the end seat when he returned.

Brent dragged the rocker forward with two short pulls.

No one commented on the new arrangement.

Seb, Marcus, Serra, and Mara followed the long bend of the sectional. Mathias sat on the short leg beside Mara. Brent remained in the rocker, now close enough to reach the table without leaning.

Mathias laid down a card and looked at Mara.

“You changed the northern lane before I found it.”

Mara’s hand paused above her cards.

“The ice?”

“The whole route.”

Serra glanced between them but continued sorting her hand.

Mara placed her card. “The route was already there.”

“It wasn’t usable.”

“It didn’t need to be. Not yet.”

“What changed?”

“The temperature along the western wall.”

Mathias frowned slightly, reconstructing it. “That pulled the sound away from the center.”

“And made the center look more important.”

“The walls looked designed to be noticed.”

“They were.” Mara leaned forward, resting her forearms against her knees. “The map kept rewarding anyone who checked them. Small details. Broken seams. Different textures. Enough to train attention toward them.”

“So you made the ice look decorative.”

“I let everyone keep assuming it was.”

Mathias traced an invisible line across the table with one finger.

“The first shelf wouldn’t have held Brent.”

From the rocker, Brent looked up. “I object to being used as the standard unit of structural failure.”

“You were the heaviest player in that section,” Mara said.

“That is better wording and the same insult.”

“The shelf only had to hold Mathias,” she continued. “Once he crossed, I dropped the outer temperature and narrowed the return.”

Mathias looked at her. “You knew I would check it.”

“I knew someone would.”

“No. You changed it after I touched the wall.”

Mara did not answer immediately.

Marcus watched the pause. Not concealment. Recalculation.

“I knew you would check again,” she said.

Mathias smiled.

Not because he had caught her. Because the answer opened another route.

“You made us change the map twice,” he said.

“You made the first version stop working.”

“That sounds almost complimentary.”

“It wasn’t meant to be discouraging.”

Serra placed a card.

“Ten.”

Mathias glanced toward her. “Are we still playing?”

“We were until you two started reconstructing terrain.”

“Bullshit,” Brent said.

Serra turned over a ten.

Brent collected the pile.

“You weren’t even looking,” she said.

“I was restoring order.”

“That isn’t the game.”

“It is now.”

Mara laughed, quiet at first, then again when Brent began explaining why prolonged tactical analysis constituted an unfair distraction.

The next story came from her before anyone asked: an Aetherfall event where the weather engine had failed and turned every interior surface into rain. That led to the first tournament she and Serra had attended together, then the hotel fire alarm that had gone off at three in the morning and Serra refusing to leave until she found both shoes.

“They were new,” Serra said.

“The building was evacuating.”

“I was evacuating correctly.”

“You were looking under the bed.”

“That was where the shoe was.”

Mathias asked how long they had lived together.

“Two years,” Mara said.

“Three in September,” Serra corrected.

Mara looked at her. “You count the sublet?”

“You paid rent.”

“You charged me forty percent.”

“You had the smaller room.”

“The room was the same size.”

“It had less emotional square footage.”

Marcus looked between them. “I’m not convinced that survives measurement.”

“It survived the lease,” Serra said.

Seb laughed and dealt the next round before the roommate dispute could acquire exhibits.

He told them about 404’s rain-soaked beach trip, which became the story of Marcus attempting to assemble a grill with enough confidence to keep everyone from checking the fuel line until smoke began emerging from the wrong side.

“The instructions were diagrammatic,” Marcus said.

“The diagram had arrows,” Brent replied.

“Several. That was part of the problem.”

Mathias insisted the restaurant from that same trip had justified the drive. Serra asked how many states they had crossed. Brent answered before anyone else could.

“Too many.”

“That isn’t a number,” Mara said.

“It is emotionally.”

“What was the restaurant?” she asked Mathias.

He named it and described a converted mill above a river in western Virginia, then the narrow road that reached it, the local farms listed on the menu, and the chef whose documentary had started the entire trip.

Brent pointed at him. “That answer is why we ended up there.”

“The food was excellent.”

“The argument has never been about the food.”

“It should be.”

Marcus turned one of his cards between his fingers. “The mistake was letting him describe the trip before giving us the distance.”

“I gave you the distance.”

“You said nearby.”

“It was nearby relative to the scope of the documentary.”

Serra laughed. “That is criminal.”

“It was geographically defensible.”

Seb began another story, this one about an older raid strategy that had survived twenty minutes of planning and less than one minute of contact.

“Micah always said if a plan survived Brent for five minutes, it was structurally sound.”

The card in Seb’s hand stopped against the table.

Only for a moment.

His eyes remained on the deck.

Brent leaned back in the rocker. “Micah also thought you cheated at anything involving cards.”

Seb looked up.

“He accused me whenever he lost.”

“He accused you before you played.”

“He understood probability.”

The exchange gave Seb somewhere to go.

He smiled, but it arrived unevenly.

“He was wrong about a lot of things.”

No one contradicted him.

Serra placed her cards facedown. “Two kings.”

Seb looked at her, then at Brent.

Brent raised both hands. “I’m staying out of this.”

“Coward,” Serra said.

“Pattern recognition.”

The game continued.

Micah’s name remained in the room, no longer hidden and not turned into a ceremony.

Later, Marcus caught Serra watching his hand rather than his face.

“You’re not reading the cards anymore,” he said.

“What am I reading?”

“The fact that I know you’re watching.”

“That sounds circular.”

“It is. That’s why it keeps working.”

She looked at him directly.

“Play.”

Marcus placed two cards.

“Queens.”

Serra called bullshit before his hand left the table.

He turned them over.

Two queens.

She pulled the pile toward herself.

Marcus frowned. “You lost.”

“You were bluffing.”

“They were queens.”

“You wanted me to call it.”

“That’s not what bluffing means.”

“It is tonight.”

He looked toward Seb.

Seb spread his hands. “Prior ruling stands.”

“Corrupt institution.”

“Stable institution,” Brent corrected.

By midnight, the game had become secondary to the room around it.

The windows had darkened fully. Their reflections hovered over the campus lights outside: six people bent toward the same table, cards scattered between them.

A nurse entered to remind them that fasting had begun and that water would stop two hours before their assigned procedures.

Brent looked at the half-empty bag of chips near his chair.

“You waited until midnight to say that?”

“It is twelve-oh-one.”

“That is a punitive interpretation of time.”

The nurse did not engage.

After she left, Seb collected the cards into a loose stack.

Nobody stood.

Brent rested his head against the rocker. “Anyone reconsidering?”

Mathias nodded. “Several times.”

“Still here.”

“So are you.”

“I’m attached to the chair now.”

Serra turned one card over on the table.

“I hate that they knew.”

No one asked what she meant.

Astria had known what to show. Not everything about them. Enough.

Mara looked at the card beneath Serra’s fingers. “They knew where to start.”

“That’s worse.”

Marcus watched Serra rotate the card once, then stop.

“They predicted the answer,” he said. “That isn’t the same as owning it.”

Her eyes moved to him.

“No,” she said. “It isn’t.”

Seb squared the deck against the table.

“Tomorrow, if something doesn’t match what they told us, say it. Don’t wait for someone else to notice.”

“Agreed,” Marcus said.

Brent nodded from the rocker. “Even if it seems minor.”

“Especially then,” Mara said. “They’re working with assumptions they haven’t shown us.”

Mathias looked around the sectional. “We compare notes as soon as we can.”

Serra set the card on top of the deck. “Then we do.”


They left the common room in stages.

Brent complained about the decorative pillows in his room. Mathias reminded him that no pillow was decorative if he was sufficiently tired. Mara accused Serra of hiding a card in her sleeve. Serra produced the missing card from beneath Mara’s folded cardigan and claimed the evidence was conclusive.

Marcus paused at his doorway and looked back once.

The cards were still on the table. The rocker remained close to the sectional. Empty glasses stood beside six different seats.

Then he went inside.

---

Someone knocked at 4:40 a.m.

Marcus woke beneath the wrong ceiling and remembered where he was before the nurse spoke through the door.

“Mr. Vye? Pre-op in twenty minutes.”

The common room had been reset during the night. Cards gone. Glasses removed. The rocker returned to its original place near the windows.

The six of them had been reduced to names and procedure times on the displays beside their doors.

Seb was already near the kitchenette drinking water. Brent sat in the rocker without moving it. Mathias emerged carrying his glasses in one hand and the cleaning cloth Astria had provided in the other.

Mara came out next in pale surgical clothing beneath a robe, her hair braided over one shoulder. Serra followed her.

Her hair had been drawn back into a single heavy braid, but the surgical cap in her hand was clearly not designed with its full length in mind.

A nurse checked their wristbands.

“Ms. Valeria, they’re ready for you in imaging review.”

Mara looked at Serra. “Text me when you wake up.”

Serra glanced down at the pocketless robe. “I’ll prioritize it.”

“I’m serious.”

“I know.”

Mara touched her forearm, then followed the nurse through the double doors.

Seb and Brent were called next. Mathias left with another technician after being told, for the second time, that his glasses could not cross the marked line on the floor.

Soon only Marcus and Serra remained near the nurses’ station.

Marcus held her gaze.

“Still revising your opinion?”

“A little.”

“Upward?”

Serra’s mouth curved. “Don’t get greedy.”

The nurse opened the surgical doors.

“Ms. Hawthorne?”

Serra stepped through, then looked back.

“See you after, Marcus.”

The doors closed behind her.

---

They took Marcus less than an hour later.

Dr. Mercer met him inside the operating suite in dark blue scrubs and a surgical cap. Without the rolled sleeves, coffee, and conference-room attempt at ease, he looked less approachable and more reliable.

“How are you feeling?” Mercer asked.

“Nervous.”

“That’s appropriate.”

Marcus looked at him. “You keep saying that like there’s a medically preferred amount.”

“There is. Enough that you understand the decision. Not enough to impair it.”

“And where am I?”

“Within range.”

Marcus accepted the classification.

A nurse confirmed his name and date of birth. Another marked the surgical site behind his right ear. The implant would sit beneath the skull near the junction of sensory integration and memory processing, with the lattice extending along structures Mercer had explained using a model Marcus had deliberately avoided imagining inside his own head.

They transferred him onto the operating table.

The room contained fewer dramatic machines than he had expected. Most of the equipment had been built into articulated arms, ceiling rails, and the walls, leaving ordinary objects to stand out more clearly: gauze, tape, clear tubing, stainless-steel trays.

An anesthesiologist attached a line to the port in Marcus’s arm.

“This may feel cold.”

It did.

Marcus looked toward Mercer. “Everyone who went ahead of me?”

“Stable,” Mercer said. “No unexpected complications.”

That was enough.

The light above him divided into three overlapping circles.

“Take a slow breath,” the anesthesiologist said.

Marcus inhaled.

The mask smelled faintly of plastic.

“Another.”

He obeyed.

The edges of the room loosened.

“Count backward from ten.”

“Ten.”

The monitor continued its steady pulse.

“Nine.”

Six lines on Mercer’s graph, drawing closer without touching.

“Eight.”

Cards scattered across a table beneath six different hands.

The room disappeared before he reached seven.

---

There was no waking.

No recovery room. No dry mouth, weight of blankets, or monitor sounding beside the bed.

Marcus became aware inside a darkness that had no relationship to closed eyes.

He possessed no body he could locate.

No hands. No breathing. No pressure beneath him. The idea of direction existed, but the dark declined to support it.

Something was nearby.

Not something.

Someone.

Then more.

Five presences held at different distances inside a space without measurement.

Seb reached first.

Marcus did not hear his voice. The words appeared whole.

*Can everyone hear me?*

A pause.

*Yes,* Serra answered.

*Mathias,* came another.

*This is a terrible recovery room,* Brent said.

Mara was there. Quieter, but unmistakable.

Marcus pushed a thought outward.

*Here.*

Six.

The recognition changed the space.

A pressure moved through the darkness, vast enough that Marcus first mistook it for the environment itself.

Then it spoke.

**Attend.**

The word did not arrive through sound. It entered beneath language and emerged already understood.

Seb answered first.

*Who are you?*

**The intermediary responsible for your passage.**

Brent’s presence tightened.

*Passage where?*

The darkness folded.

An image opened beneath them, or around them, too large to belong to any single direction.

A city stood where a broad river met a dark sea. Stone walls enclosed miles of dense roofs, canals, bridges, and crowded streets. Docks pushed into the water in long rows, thick with ships. Roads passed through gates and spread across the plain beyond, dividing fields, villages, and smaller towns before continuing toward a horizon crowded with mountains.

Far inland, beyond the city and its cultivated ground, another settlement clung to older ruins. Broken stone rose around a fractured central mass. A narrow violet light climbed from it into the storm-dark sky.

The image vanished before Marcus could decide whether he had seen the city from above or remembered someone else seeing it.

**Eriadne.**

The name remained after everything else disappeared.

Serra pushed against the pressure.

*You’re sending us there?*

**I will attempt to place you near it.**

The word *attempt* carried more weight than the rest.

Mathias caught it too.

*How near?*

**The passage allows direction, not precision. One of you may arrive within sight of the walls. Another may be hundreds of miles away. I cannot know until you cross.**

*Can you keep us together?* Mara asked.

**No.**

The answer held no cruelty. Only limitation.

Seb pressed forward.

*Then tell us what we need to do.*

**Survive the arrival. Travel toward Eriadne. Find one another there.**

Marcus tried to find the edge of whatever was speaking. There was no edge.

*What did Astria put in us?*

The vast attention turned toward him.

**An interpreter.**

*For what?*

**For a world your minds were not formed to read directly. It will render what it can into usable terms. What it cannot render will remain beyond you.**

No revelation followed. No sudden understanding. Only the statement and the dark around it.

Mathias asked, *What happens if we arrive too far away?*

**You travel farther.**

Brent’s response came sharp.

*That’s your plan?*

**It is the condition.**

Marcus felt the others react to the distinction in different ways. Serra pressing against it. Mara going still. Mathias turning the wording over. Seb searching for the part that could become action. Brent rejecting the premise without finding leverage against it.

The darkness tightened around all six of them.

For one moment, Marcus felt the same structure Mercer’s graph had shown: separate points answering one another across a space that should not have connected them.

Then something opened.

Not a door.

The possibility of falling.

**Reach Eriadne.**

One presence vanished.

Then another.

The dark emptied by degrees, each absence leaving no direction in which to follow.

Marcus reached for the remaining presences and found nothing he could hold.

Then he was alone.

The darkness opened beneath him.

Gravity found him all at once.
