# Eriadne — The Thread City

> **Status:** Active settlement — Book 1 primary location
> **Last updated:** working draft — redesigned from earlier version

---

## The Name

Eriadne is a deliberate variant of Ariadne — associated with the thread, the labyrinth, and the guide who helps others find their way through. The name is not coincidence. Whether the city was named deliberately by people who knew what lay beneath it, or whether the name accreted to a place that earned it over centuries, is unresolved and probably unknowable.

What the name means for the story:
- The place of the thread
- The city where paths converge
- The guide-point through the labyrinth
- The knot where hidden systems become visible

Marcus is an ocular/interface user who sees connections others cannot. Eriadne is the city of threads. He belongs here before he understands why.

---

## How Eriadne Became What It Is

Eriadne was not built. It accumulated.

The ruins came first — ancient structures on the outskirts of what is now the city, left behind by a civilization that no longer exists. For a long time they were simply ruins: dangerous, occasionally explored by treasure hunters, mostly ignored. The ruins sat at a location that turned out to be significant for reasons that had nothing to do with treasure — a natural crossroads where several major trade and travel routes converged, and beneath the surface, the **convergence of the eight elemental ley lines** — which holds a dormant natural portal — whose full significance most people never understood.

The settlement came because of the crossroads. Traders needed a stopping point. Then merchants built permanent stalls. Then craftsmen followed the merchants. Then people who served the craftsmen. Then people who had nowhere else to go and found that this particular crossroads was easier to survive near than most places. The ruins on the outskirts drew their own visitors — historians, scavengers, the curious, the desperate. Some of those visitors stayed.

No one planned Eriadne. No one founded it. It filled in over the ruins and around the crossroads the way water fills low ground — because the geography made it easier to be here than not to be here.

The result is a city that feels organic because it is: roads that follow old animal paths, buildings that incorporate ruin-stone because it was available, districts that exist because a certain kind of person kept ending up in the same part of town. Nothing matches. Everything works, after a fashion.

## Physical Shape

Eriadne is not a walled city, not a fortress city, not a city built to a plan. It sprawls. The oldest and densest settlement clusters near the center, where the original crossroads sat. From there it thins outward in irregular fingers — denser along the old roads, sparse in the gaps between them.

The ruins sit on the outskirts. Not surrounded by the city, not beneath it — adjacent to it, at the edges where the settlement thins out and the old structures begin. This edge-zone is where the relationship between Eriadne and its ruins is most visible: buildings that butt up against ancient walls, streets that dead-end at sealed doorways too large to be residential, foundations of current structures that turn out to be the tops of older ones.

Most residents treat the ruins as background. They are there. They have always been there. You do not go into them unless you know what you are doing, and even then you may not come back out.

### The Trade District
The center of Eriadne, where the crossroads originally sat. Dense, loud, permanent. Markets, inns, craftsmen, money-changers. The part of the city that outsiders see and take to be the whole thing.

### The Residential Districts
Multiple distinct neighborhoods that grew up around the trade center, each with its own character and informal identity. Some are old enough to have multi-generational families. Some are recent enough that residents still think of themselves as temporary.

### The Outer Districts
Where the city gets sparse. Newer arrivals, refugees, people who couldn't afford the center. More transient, less organized, fewer of the informal social agreements that hold the inner city together. This is also where the ruins start.

### The Ruin-Fringe
The edge-zone where city meets ruins. Some structures here are hybrid — occupied buildings that incorporate ancient stonework. Others are clearly ruins that no one currently lives in but that show signs of recent activity. Treasure hunters, scholars, the desperate, and the curious all pass through.

This is where Marcus establishes his first foothold. Not in the city proper — in the ruin-fringe that the city has written off.

## The Ley-Line Convergence

Beneath Eriadne — and specifically beneath the ruins on its outskirts — the **eight elemental ley lines** converge. This is not widely understood. Most residents know the city has a certain energy: magic works differently here, certain places feel wrong, the ruins have a reputation that exceeds their visible danger. The academic understanding of what is actually happening underground belongs to very few people.

The ruins were built by an ancient civilization that understood the convergence and built deliberately on top of it. What they built it for — transit hub, gate lock, regulatory mechanism, weapon, archive — is unknown. The ruins are partially active in ways that suggest the original system was never fully shut down, only abandoned.

The convergence holds a **dormant natural portal** at its knot — not built, but *formed* by the eight elemental lines meeting; the ancient builders raised their structures around it. **At the Book-1 finale this portal is forced open and a Xyloryn Myrmidon comes *through* it; the breach itself draws a Realm Walker, who witnesses the fight and makes first contact with Marcus shortly after** (→ `cosmology.md`, `master_timeline.md`). Who or what forces it open is unresolved (**W-05**).

The ley-line convergence is why Eriadne sits where it does. Not because the founders chose this location for strategic reasons — they chose it because it was a trade crossroads. The convergence is underneath the reason they came, older than the reason, indifferent to it.

**Marcus's relationship to it:** His ocular interface reads ley-line structures. When he arrives in Eriadne and first enters the ruin-fringe, the ruins begin responding to his presence before he understands why. The system underneath recognizes something in his interface.

## Governance — The Absence of Authority

Eriadne has no formal governance. No mayor, no council with actual power, no guard force with real jurisdiction. It is a frontier town that grew very large without ever developing the institutions that cities usually build as they grow.

What exists instead:

**Neighborhood compacts** — informal agreements between blocks and districts about who handles what and where the lines are. Unwritten, enforced socially.

**The Merchants' Accord** — the closest thing to a city-wide institution. A loose coalition of traders who agreed on weights, currency exchange, and commercial dispute resolution. Has leverage, not authority.

**The Wardens** — a volunteer patrol that started in one neighborhood and extended outward over time. Not a police force. A community watch that developed muscle memory for the city's specific problems. Quality varies by district.

**Old families** — names that mean something because they've been here long enough. No formal power. Significant social weight.

**What this means for Marcus:** There is no authority to petition and no authority to oppose. Power in Eriadne is maintained constantly through relationships, reputation, and credible consequences. He becomes relevant by becoming useful to people the existing nodes ignore.

## The Ruins

The ruins that drew the first settlers and that continue to draw treasure hunters, scholars, and the desperate are on the outskirts of the city — the ruin-fringe. They are not buried beneath Eriadne so much as adjacent to it, incorporated into it at the edges.

What is known about the ruins:
- They predate the current settlement by a significant margin — potentially by thousands of years
- The stonework is unlike anything currently produced in the region
- Certain sections of the ruins are warm when they should be cold
- Some rooms have geometry that does not match their floor plans
- Several access points to deeper levels exist; most are sealed, some are not
- People who go too deep sometimes do not come back; sometimes they come back changed

What the ruins contain — the full scope of what the ancient civilization built here — is unknown. The surface-accessible portions have been picked over for centuries. Whatever remains significant is deeper in.

Marcus's claim on the ruin-fringe is not strategic at first. He needs a place that is not already someone else's. He sees usefulness in a space others have written off. The ley-line response to his presence is something he notices without yet understanding.

## Sensory Profile

**Sound:** Eriadne is never quiet. Market noise bleeds into residential districts at all hours. The ruin-fringe is quieter — not silent, but the sounds from the city feel more distant than the distance justifies. In certain parts of the ruins, sound behaves slightly wrong: echoes that arrive a half-beat late, directions that don't match the source.

**Smell:** Trade district — people, animals, cookfires, spice, dung, metal. Residential — woodsmoke, cooking, the specific smell of a neighborhood that has been inhabited for a long time. Ruin-fringe — the city smells fade and something underneath comes through: mineral cold, old stone, occasionally something that has no name and that people who live near the ruins have learned to not think about.

**Feel:** Dense at the center, looser toward the edges. The ruin-fringe has a specific quality underfoot — the ground does not always feel solid in the way it should. In certain locations near ley-line access points, the soles of the feet register something that is not vibration but resembles it.

**Look:** No consistent architecture. Every era of Eriadne's growth is visible in the buildings — different stone, different mortar, different construction methods layered on top of each other. The ruins on the outskirts are visually distinct: larger blocks, different angles, stonework that was built to last longer than the civilization that built it has.

## W-01 — The Builders, or a Corpse? (PROPOSED alternative — not locked)

The current **builder** framing (an ancient civilization raised structures around the natural convergence) **stays in place but is flagged disliked / for-replacement.** The **leading proposed alternative** (unlocked — do not assert): the "ruins" are not architecture at all but the dormant **corpse of a N'hal** — *structure = anatomy, not masonry; the portal = a wound / aperture, not a built gate.*

If adopted, the implications (captured, not asserted — all **PROPOSED**):
- **(i)** The ruins "recognizing" Marcus reframes to: this is the one place his interface shows him the **seams** — glitches that *mean* something; "**interface ≠ world**" rendered as geography.
- **(ii)** A dead N'hal is the **Nightbound founder's *evidentiary* proof**, and the reason the Realm "registered" to the N'hal — the civilization that **killed** it is the one that "prepared for the wrong war."
- **(iii)** "People come back changed" from the deep ruins = **residual denaturing.**
- **(iv)** The finale agent forces open the **corpse's wound-aperture** (the breach).

**Dosage:** even if adopted, the corpse is present on-page as a dead *place* — **not *named* N'hal until Book 2** (preserves the "distant weather" rule). Tracked as **W-01** (`unresolved_character_threads.md`, `master_timeline.md`, `cosmology.md` → N'hal).
