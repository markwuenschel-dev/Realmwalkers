# Forbidden Drift — Dominion Realm

> **Purpose:** Specific named drift patterns that indicate the prose has left the story's intended lane. Each pattern has a name, a description, warning signs, and a correction direction. Read before drafting. Use as a diagnostic when a scene feels off.
>
> Cross-reference `genre_boundaries.md` for the tonal walls. `voice_guide.md` for the intended register.

---

## The Drift Patterns

### 1. Horror-Author Pastiche

**What it is:** The prose starts imitating the surface features of literary horror — extended dread, gothic atmosphere, elaborate sensory detail of wrongness — without the underlying character interiority that would make it earned. It sounds like it's trying to be Stephen King rather than being itself.

**Warning signs:**
- Three or more sentences of atmospheric dread in a row with no character reaction
- The Realm's strangeness described at length without Marcus noticing or processing it
- Dread that accumulates through adjectives rather than through specific detail that matters
- The prose enjoying the wrongness rather than the character experiencing it

**Correction:** Return to Marcus's interiority. What specifically does he notice and why? What does it remind him of? What does he do with the information? The atmosphere should be a byproduct of his perception, not a separate layer dropped over the scene.

---

### 2. Generic Grimdark

**What it is:** Violence, suffering, and moral bleakness accumulate as signals of seriousness rather than as consequences of story. The prose wallows. Characters are defined primarily by their damage. The tone implies that darkness equals depth.

**Warning signs:**
- Violence described with more detail than the scene's emotional stakes justify
- Characters who have no warmth, humor, or humanity left
- Suffering that doesn't connect to a specific character's arc or choice
- The implicit message that hope is naive
- Aftermath scenes that dwell rather than land and move

**Correction:** Restore consequence without aestheticizing it. Violence should cost something specific. The aftermath should be felt through behavior, not described at length. Keep at least one thing in each scene that the character still cares about.

---

### 3. Anime Monologue Inflation

**What it is:** Characters — especially Marcus — deliver speeches about their own psychology, power, and motivations that no person would actually say in that moment. Internal monologue inflates to the point where action stops entirely while Marcus explains himself to himself at length.

**Warning signs:**
- Internal monologue running more than half a page before anything happens
- Marcus explaining his own emotional state in complete analytical sentences at a moment of crisis
- Characters articulating their character arc clearly and directly ("I realize now that I've been using humor to avoid feeling vulnerable")
- Villain explains plan. Hero counters with philosophy. Both have time for this during combat.

**Correction:** Trust behavior over statement. Marcus's psychology should be visible in what he does and notices, not in what he says about himself. Internal monologue during action should be fragmentary — compressed thoughts, not essays. Save the longer introspective beats for quiet aftermath scenes.

---

### 4. Cozy Fantasy Softness

**What it is:** The stakes and danger of the Realm soften until the world feels comfortable and consequence-free. Characters are too uniformly kind. Problems resolve without real cost. The tone becomes warm in a way that undercuts tension.

**Warning signs:**
- Every NPC Marcus meets is helpful and trustworthy
- Combat resolves cleanly with no injury or meaningful cost
- The ruined district and its residents feel charming rather than desperate
- Eriadne's political danger evaporates when characters talk it out
- Humor consistently lightens moments that should remain heavy

**Correction:** Restore friction. Not every person Marcus meets should be safe. Not every problem should be solvable through charm. The warmth in the story should be earned and specific — a moment of genuine connection against a background of real danger — not the default register of every scene.

---

### 5. Meme-Humor Spam / Marvel Quip Saturation

**What it is:** Humor becomes constant and detached from stakes. Every tense moment is punctured by a quip. Marcus narrates his own situation with ironic distance. Pop culture references substitute for specific observation. The voice signals that nothing is quite serious.

**Warning signs:**
- More than two jokes in a scene that is supposed to be tense
- Quips that arrive before the character has earned the right to be funny (he's terrified but cracks wise anyway with no acknowledgment of the terror)
- Pop culture references — especially stacked ones — used to establish Marcus's voice
- The humor creates distance from the scene rather than living inside it
- Ayla's dry responses start to feel like a comedy duo setup

**Correction:** Space the humor. Marcus's playful chaos is most effective when it arrives unexpectedly against a backdrop of genuine tension. One well-placed, specific joke beats three generic ones. The humor should feel like stress response, not performance. Refer to the original draft's mindscape sequence — the chin-raise while being choked works because it's surrounded by genuine horror.

---

### 6. Encyclopedia Exposition

**What it is:** The prose stops to explain the Realm — its history, its politics, its magic systems, its factions — in ways that prioritize reader information over character experience. The world is told rather than encountered.

**Warning signs:**
- Ayla delivers a paragraph explaining how ley lines work
- Marcus thinks through faction politics in complete sentences that convey information he wouldn't actually think through this way
- A new location is described comprehensively before Marcus has had a chance to notice anything about it
- The prose summarizes worldbuilding in the narrative voice rather than filtering it through a character

**Correction:** Everything the reader learns about the Realm should come through Marcus's encounter with it. He notices one thing. He misunderstands it. Ayla corrects him incompletely. He draws a wrong conclusion. This is slower but it makes the world feel real rather than documented. See `prose_contract.md` Rule 5.

---

### 7. System-Message Spam

**What it is:** LitRPG notifications appear so frequently that they interrupt scene momentum, replace sensory description, and begin to feel like the story is a game walkthrough with prose in between.

**Warning signs:**
- More than two system notifications in a single scene that isn't a tutorial sequence
- Notifications explaining things the scene has already shown through prose
- System messages appearing during emotional beats where they undercut rather than complicate
- Stat panels appearing mid-action
- The reader needs to read the notification to understand what just happened (the prose wasn't doing its job)

**Correction:** The prose should carry the scene. The notification interrupts it — briefly, meaningfully, and at the wrong moment in the right way. See `system_message_rules.md` for the full protocol. In mid-to-late story, ask whether a notification is earning its place. If removing it makes the scene cleaner, remove it.

---

### 8. Sudden Literary Detachment

**What it is:** The prose suddenly shifts to a cooler, more distant register — more formally literary, more observational, less inside Marcus's head — in a way that feels like a different author took over. Usually happens when a scene is trying to be "serious" or "beautiful" and overcorrects away from the established voice.

**Warning signs:**
- Sentences that are beautifully constructed but could belong to any literary novel
- Marcus disappears from the scene and the prose floats above events
- Emotional moments rendered in abstract rather than specific terms
- The voice sounds more polished than Marcus would be
- "The world felt different after that." type endings that summarize rather than land.

**Correction:** Return to the specific. What does Marcus notice? What does his body do? What thought does he have that he can't quite complete? The voice should feel slightly rougher and more immediate than literary prose — still controlled, but not polished to the point of distance.

---

### 9. Over-Polished Voice Erasure

**What it is:** Line editing or revision passes smooth the prose until Marcus's personality disappears. The sentences are correct. The voice is gone. The story reads like competent anonymous fiction.

**Warning signs:**
- No unfinished thoughts in internal monologue
- No humor anywhere in the scene
- Every sentence is grammatically complete and appropriately weighted
- The prose could have been written by anyone
- Marcus sounds like a narrator, not a person

**Correction:** Re-introduce Marcus's specific tics. He thinks in partial sentences under pressure. He finds the absurd detail in serious situations. He notices the wrong things when he's scared. He makes a structural observation when he should be feeling something. These are not errors to correct — they are characterization.

---

### 10. Serra Flattening

**What it is:** Serra loses her independent agenda and becomes defined primarily by her relationship to Marcus — waiting for him, supporting him, being in danger so he can act, or existing to advance his emotional arc.

**Warning signs:**
- Serra's POV scenes are mostly about Marcus
- Her decisions are reactions to what Marcus does rather than driven by her own goals
- She expresses vulnerability specifically to create an emotional moment for Marcus
- Her solo arc stops being shown and is summarized in ways that make it feel secondary
- She becomes warmer and more available the longer the story progresses

**Correction:** Every Serra scene should have something she wants that has nothing to do with Marcus. Her arc — the hunt, the wrong she walked into, what it's doing to her — is its own thing. The Marcus/Serra dynamic is important precisely because they have separate lives that occasionally intersect. If her scenes could be removed without losing anything except relationship content, they need more of their own weight.

---

### 11. Deleted Middle / Referred Action

**What it is:** The prose hands the reader the *result* or *verdict* of an action whose middle was never shown. It refers to an event instead of staging it; it lets a metaphor stand in for a literal thing the reader hasn't seen; it introduces a mechanism and exploits it in the same breath. The reader is asked to reconstruct frames that aren't on the page — and can't. This is the most common failure in early drafts and the one that makes a scene "impossible to follow" even when every sentence is well-made.

**Warning signs:**
- A sentence states the *outcome* of a physical action whose steps weren't rendered ("she planted on the wall and his block made it worse")
- A phrase points at a specific event the reader was never shown, as if they watched it ("the other side of that exact decision")
- A metaphor carries meaning the literal scene hasn't earned ("it's a gun" before any coercion is concrete)
- A rule, power, or limitation is named and exploited in the same moment, so the reader can't see the trap close
- The reader has to infer a step to make the sentence parse

**Correction:** Stage the event in plain physical sequence — what moved, where, what resulted — before any interpretation. Define a mechanism before the beat that exploits it. Establish the normal before you break it. Let metaphor *color* a shown action, never *replace* an unseen one. Full procedure and per-rule checks: `prose_clarity_rules.md`. Worked before/after: `style_examples.md` #11.

**Note — this is not the same as compression.** Compressing choreography in favor of interiority is correct when the interior cost is the point (`prose_contract.md` Rule 1, interiority-load-bearing mode). Deleted Middle is the *failure* version: compressing away physical steps the reader needs to follow what happened. The line between them is whether the choreography carries meaning. When in doubt, run `prose_clarity_rules.md`'s One Test.

### 12. Axis Collapse (Relational Over-Correction)

**What it is:** A relationship true on two independent axes gets flattened to one — usually under correction pressure, when a fix to one axis silently deletes the other. The signature is an agent retiring a *true* half along with the false half it was told to fix.

**Warning signs:**
- A correction phrased "X is wrong, it's Y" produces a draft where X is gone even though X was half-true
- Parity and recognition treated as one quantity ("recognized each other as equals")
- A FIXED standing depicted as acquired on-screen; an EARNED standing present at full strength from the first beat
- A one-directional dynamic written as mutual

**Correction:** Re-separate the axes per `relationship_invariants.md` — FIXED as already true, EARNED in motion. Run `relational_clarity_rules.md`. Reveal, don't confer.

### 13. Pole Overshoot

**What it is:** Fixing one failure pole drives the scene into the opposite one — correcting "Marcus dominates Serra" yields "Marcus is the underdog who levels up to her." Both are wrong; the target is between them.

**Warning signs:**
- The scene is clean of the pole just corrected and guilty of its opposite
- A character who was too strong is now too weak (or vice versa) against a FIXED-parity invariant

**Correction:** Name both poles before revising (`relationship_invariants.md`, "Both poles"). Aim *for* the invariant, not *away* from the last error.

---

### 14. Under-Rendered Combat / Summarized Choreography

**What it is:** A fight is in choreography-load-bearing mode but delivered as verdicts — entrances skipped, sub-exchanges summarized, state-changes free and invisible, outcomes asserted. The scene is *about* the physical event and never stages it. The combat-specific face of Deleted Middle (#11), and the one #11 kept failing to catch because it reads as an exemplar, not a checklist.

**Warning signs:**
- An actor first appears mid-effect ("she's in" → already attacking)
- "they traded for a minute," "he survived it," "the graze" — exchanges as results
- Form/stance/position changes with no rendered look and no cost
- "overperformed / shouldn't have survived / dominated" with no mechanic under it
- The Critic cited #11 to *approve* thin combat instead of diagramming it

**Correction:** Run The One Test as a hard gate and walk R9–R14 (`prose_clarity_rules.md`). The Critic must produce a beat-by-beat reconstruction; any unreconstructable load-bearing beat is CHANGES REQUESTED.

## Quick Diagnostic

When a scene feels off, run through this list:

| Drift Type | Quick Test |
|---|---|
| Horror pastiche | Is atmosphere carrying scenes instead of character interiority? |
| Generic grimdark | Is darkness accumulating as aesthetic rather than consequence? |
| Anime monologue | Did anyone explain their own psychology out loud for more than two sentences? |
| Cozy softness | Is every problem resolving without real cost? |
| Meme-humor spam | Are there more than two jokes in a tense scene? |
| Encyclopedia exposition | Did the prose explain the Realm rather than have a character encounter it? |
| System-message spam | Could the scene be stronger if some notifications were cut? |
| Literary detachment | Did Marcus disappear from his own narrative? |
| Over-polished erasure | Does the prose sound like it was written by anyone? |
| Serra flattening | Does Serra have something she wants that has nothing to do with Marcus? |
| Deleted middle | Can a reader reconstruct the physical event from the page, or are they handed the result of an unseen action? |
| Axis collapse | Is any two-axis relationship being treated as one quantity? |
| Pole overshoot | Did fixing one failure pole land the scene in the opposite pole? |
| Under-rendered combat | Can you diagram the exchange beat-by-beat from the prose? Any load-bearing beat off-page or summarized? |
