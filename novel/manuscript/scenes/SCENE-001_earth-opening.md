# Aim Not Found

> **Scene goal (one line):** Establish Marcus's ordinary world — ML engineer by day, serious competitor by night — and bring the night to its real test: a duel with the best killer in the bracket, cut short the instant Astria's hijack lands.
> **Entering state:** Marcus is competent, bored, and quietly unsettled by a result at work he can't make stop happening. The night's only real stake is a scrim he wants to win.
> **Exiting state:** Mid-duel with Serra, the squad's whole plan made physical against one player — and the monitors snow over. The first wrongness lands. Nothing is resolved.

---

The model was lying to him, and it was being polite about it.

That was the part Marcus kept circling back to, the part that didn't sit right even after he'd stared at it long enough to lose the meaning of the numbers. A model that's broken tells you it's broken. It throws errors, it diverges, it produces garbage that any junior could flag in the first review. This one wasn't doing that. This one was producing beautiful, confident, well-calibrated predictions about a group of people it had been specifically built not to touch, and it was getting them right.

He pulled up the control cohort again.

The whole point of a control cohort was that you left it alone. You build a model to predict — aptitude, retention, performance, whatever the contract called for that quarter — and you carve off a slice of the population and you don't feed their outcomes back in. You don't optimize against them. They're the flat surface you lay your ruler against to find out if the rest of your work is bent. The control group is supposed to drift. It's supposed to be noisy, ordinary, indifferent to anything you do, because you never did anything to it.

His control group was converging.

Not toward the average, which would have been one kind of wrong — a leak, a contaminated feature, somebody upstream feeding it data it shouldn't have. Marcus knew what a leak looked like. He'd found three of them this year, two of them his own. A leak made the model too good in a way that smelled like cheating the second you traced it back.

This wasn't that. He'd traced it back. He'd spent the better part of two weeks tracing it back, and the wires all went where they were supposed to go and stopped where they were supposed to stop. The control cohort was sealed. Nothing he'd built touched it. Nothing he'd built *could* touch it.

And yet, month over month, the people in it were arriving at outcomes the model would have predicted for them — if the model had been allowed to act on them. Which it hadn't. Which it couldn't. It was as if someone outside the system was reaching in and arranging the results to match, quietly, after the fact, and the model was sitting there taking the credit with a straight face.

*Something else is doing the work,* he thought, *and I'm getting paid for it.*

He saved the notebook. He did not write that sentence down anywhere a quarterly review would find it.

Across the open floor, somebody's monitor was still running the company stream on mute — the launch was three days out and the marketing team had been playing the teaser on loop like an incantation. Astria's logo dissolving into a render of the thing they'd built, the processor that was going to *reshape the very fabric of our lives*, the phrase scrolling under it in the typeface they'd paid a great deal of money to make look inevitable. Marcus had heard the line enough times that it had stopped meaning anything, the way a word does when you say it too long. He didn't work on the device. Nobody he knew worked on the device. It had come out of a part of the building you needed a different badge to enter, and the people who had that badge did not eat lunch with the people who didn't.

He shut his laptop. The anomaly would still be there tomorrow. Anomalies were patient that way. It was the one thing he liked about them.

His phone buzzed. Seb, in the guild channel: *lobby in 40. tell me ur leaving the office.*

Marcus typed *leaving the office* and stood up before it could become a lie.

---

The apartment was a place he passed through on the way to better rooms. He'd lived in it for four years and could have described it to a stranger in two sentences. One of those sentences would have been about the rig.

He ate standing up — half a leftover something he didn't taste, because eating was a tax on the time before the lobby and he paid it as fast as possible — and then he did the only ritual he had left that he actually believed in. He checked the rig. Headset cabled and clean. The capture chair, the genuinely absurd thing he'd financed over eighteen months and refused to feel guilty about, its wrist and ankle cuffs hanging open like something out of a procedure he'd consented to. Sensors that read the lean of his shoulders, the shift of his weight, the half-twitch of a hand reaching for a weapon he didn't physically own — and never quite the same weapon twice. The chair turned his body into an input. That was the whole pitch, and it was the only pitch in his life he'd ever bought without arguing.

At the office he spent his days proving that human behavior was predictable enough to model. Here, for a few hours a night, he got to be the variable the model couldn't solve for.

He didn't think of it as a hobby. He'd had hobbies; this wasn't one. The guild was twenty-five in the world and clawing, and he was the part of the plan they couldn't show the other teams in advance. There was a championship in July with a number attached to it that he tried not to say out loud because saying it made his hands stupid. None of that was why he played. He played because in the lobby nothing was steering the outcome but the thirty people standing in it, and after the day he'd had, the cleanliness of that was almost unbearable.

He sat. He fitted the cuffs. He logged in.

---

There was always a half-second where the world arrived before he did — the headset rendering the loading dock around him while his sense of his own body caught up and folded into the avatar. He came in standing on cold gray plate, in the long industrial throat of the staging warehouse, the place the game called the lobby and everyone else called the only room that mattered. Banks of monitors lined the upper walls, dark for now, waiting. At the far end the cargo doors stood shut, two stories of them, the seam down the middle bright with the light of a battlefield they hadn't been let into yet.

Twenty-nine other people were already here, scattered in clusters by squad, their armor reading wrong for the room the way it always did — knights and mages and a man in matte-black plate standing in a warehouse. Marcus had stopped finding it strange somewhere in year one. Now it was just where he worked.

The man in black plate turned the instant he loaded in, which meant Seb had been watching the spawn point for him specifically.

"There he is." Seb's avatar didn't have a face you could read through the visor, but four years had taught Marcus to hear the grin in it. "I was starting to draft the eulogy. 'He died as he lived — almost on time.'"

"I'm exactly on time." Marcus rolled his shoulders, plate settling on plate without a seam between the wanting and the moving. "You're just early because you're nervous."

"I'm not nervous, I'm *prepared.* There's a difference and you'd know it if you read the brief." But Seb said it warm, the way he said everything, and clapped a gauntlet on Marcus's pauldron as he steered them both toward the squad. With Seb you were always being steered somewhere, and the trick of him was that you didn't mind, because where he was steering you was usually toward the version of the night where everybody won. *We've got this,* his whole body said, before he'd said anything at all. *We, all of us, together — watch.*

The squad was already half-formed when they reached it. Brent first, because Brent was always first, leaning against a support strut with the deliberate boredom of a man conserving something. He ran support — the squad's medic — which had never matched the rest of him; you expected someone gentle and got a guy who treated triage like a hand tool, the way other people treated a wrench.

"Headset's hot," Brent said, not looking up. "Both your screens are clean and your left arm's reading a hair slow. You'll want to know that before you blame the game."

"It's reading fine."

"It's reading a hair slow. I'm just telling you where the body's buried." He finally glanced over, and that was Brent — bluntness that read as rude until you understood it was the closest he came to looking out for you.

Mathias was crouched a little apart, the way the Rangers always ended up, half in the squad and half watching the room breathe. He waited a beat after Marcus arrived before he spoke, the pause that newcomers mistook for slowness and the rest of them had learned to read as Mathias deciding the thing was worth saying.

"They've stopped milling," he said. "Dead Hand. Over the last few minutes. A team that's ready spreads out — finds its corners. They've been pooling toward their leader and not moving." He said it the way he said most things, like he was reading weather off a tree line. "Something's not settled over there. I'd want to know what before the doors open."

"Maybe their internet's bad." That was Kip, broad even by frontline standards, already shifting his weight from foot to foot like the fight was a held breath he was tired of holding. Kip didn't do waiting. "Maybe we stop reading their tea leaves and remember we're better than them. We swarm the casters, we collapse the line, we go home twenty-five and zero on the night." He grinned at Marcus. "And the secret weapon does the secret weapon thing."

"The secret weapon would love to know what the plan actually is," Marcus said, "before he's expected to be a surprise."

Seb pulled them in. The warmth folded back a degree — not gone, just managed, the way it always went when he had thirty people to point in one direction.

"Here's the night," he said. "We don't out-boss them. We've tried out-bossing the top teams and we keep dying in the quarters because the best guilds fight the boss and each other at the same time and we can only do one at once. So tonight we don't split our attention. We hunt." He let that sit. "We swarm their casters first, while their frontline's still committed forward. We surround Kip's opposite number and we wipe the team off the map, and *then* we walk to the boss like it's a formality. We learn whether we can do that against people who hit back this hard." A gauntlet, briefly, on Marcus's shoulder again. "Which is where he comes in. But only once it's working."

"That's a player-kill night," Brent said. It wasn't an objection. With Brent it was a tool being named so everyone knew which one was in his hand. "Against the best killers in the bracket. They've got a double-assassin core that reads pressure better than our own people do."

"Had," Mathias said quietly.

Everyone looked at him.

"They've got one assassin warming up over there," he said. "Where's the other one?"

The overhead system answered first, because the game didn't care about their timing. The voice came down out of the warehouse ceiling, metallic, indifferent, the herald it always was.

> THE SCENARIO WILL BEGIN IN TEN MINUTES.

It rolled through the room and left it quieter. Across thirty avatars Marcus watched the gear-check ripple go through — the small adjustments, the last reseating of a grip, the way even people pretending to be loose suddenly weren't. He felt his own pulse change, the avatar and the warehouse and the held breath of him all settling around the same simple animal fact: soon, now.

This was the part he'd traded a normal life for. Not the trophy. This. The minute before a thing he'd practiced enough to deserve.

"So we're really doing the kill route," Kip said, and now even he sounded like he'd swallowed some of the room's stillness. "Against *them.*"

"We're doing it," Seb said, "because we'd rather find out tonight than in front of a stadium." He almost made it sound like comfort. With Seb it nearly always did.

The minutes burned down. Marcus ran his own checklist the way he ran everything — quietly, completely, the same discipline that lived in his work and could not be turned off when he left it. Cooldowns. Sightlines off the spawn. The two routes to the enemy casters and which one he'd take if Kip's wall went up late. He was somewhere in the middle of it when the monitors along the upper wall flickered, all at once, and went to snow.

> THE SCENARIO WILL BEGIN IN FIVE MINUTES.

The snow resolved into a face. Plated visor, a narrow slit of a man inside it — the Dead Hand leader, patched in from the identical warehouse on the other side of the seam. The room turned to look up at him the way you look at a referee.

"We're set," the leader said. "Or we will be. Last seat's still empty. We thought she wasn't going to make it." A pause, and the visor turned, looking at something off the edge of his own feed. "Hold. There she is."

The view behind him split. The leader's monitor was a window into the far warehouse, the mirror of this one across the seam, and along the bottom of it ran the Dead Hand roster — thirty slots, twenty-nine lit, one dark. Marcus watched the dark one because the leader had told him where to look, and because some part of him had been watching for it since Mathias asked where the other assassin was.

The empty slot flipped. Not AWOL, not a slow yellow connecting-state crawling toward live — it went from dark to a hard, steady green in a single frame, the way a light goes on when someone who knows the room hits the switch on the first reach. And on the far spawn pad, in the back corner of the leader's feed, an avatar resolved into the world. Lean, dark, economical. No load-in stagger, no glance around the room to find her bearings, no test-swing to settle into the body. She arrived already standing the way the rest of them stood after a minute of warming up, took two steps off the pad to clear it for nobody, and stopped. Set her feet. Went still in a way that read, even at this distance, even through a monitor, as a person who had finished the part of this that took thinking and was now only waiting for the door.

Marcus had spent a season on her tape and he knew that stillness. It was the absence of every wasted thing — no shift of weight, no reseating of a grip, none of the small human noise the other twenty-nine were making right now without knowing it. She had logged into a fight she'd decided the shape of before her feet touched the pad.

"She's in," the leader said, unnecessarily, because the whole room had watched it. A short laugh, the kind that's mostly relief. "Never the one we wait on, that one. Sorry to nobody." He shook his head. "So. We're all here. Open it."

"Who was that?" Seb asked, though Marcus already knew, the way the whole squad already knew.

"Our rogue."

And Marcus felt it land in the squad before anyone moved — a small collective recalibration, the same one he felt in himself. *Their* rogue. Serra, in the guild tags. The one half the bracket had film on and none of it had helped, the killer who committed past the point a careful player bailed, like the commitment was the whole plan, and made you watch yourself lose the exchange before you'd picked which way to lose it. The reason they'd built the entire night around the kill route was to find out if they could survive a player like that with the trophy on the line.

He'd spent a season on her tape. He could have picked her out of a lineup by the rhythm of her openers alone. To the rest of the bracket she was a weather event; to him she was the benchmark, the player you measured your own ceiling against, and he had never once been in the room when she lost. The whole expensive point of the scrim had just walked onto her pad on the far side of the seam, late, sorry to nobody, and ready.

"Open it," Seb told the screen, and the visor tipped, and the monitors went dark, and somewhere a clock that mattered started running.

The cargo doors came apart down their bright seam with a sound that lived in Marcus's chest more than his ears.

---

The first ninety seconds went exactly the way Seb had drawn them, which was its own kind of warning. 404 didn't fold left to take the boss. They folded sideways, into the gap between the Dead Hand frontline and its casters, and Kip's wall went up clean and on time and the line bent the way a line bends when it's been surprised by something it studied tape on. Brent's marks bloomed over the squad in pale chains. Seb's voice ran underneath all of it, even, collective, *here, here, now here.* They were swarming the casters. The plan was a plan and not a hope.

Marcus rotated as he moved, the way he always did, his class refusing to be one thing. He came off the spawn as Sentinel because Sentinel was slow and heavy and good for being a problem somebody had to solve, and the first Dead Hand bruiser who tried to peel toward Brent broke against him and went nowhere. Then Predator for two seconds to cross the open ground faster than anything that size should, and then he was Arbiter, balanced, reading, the form he thought in. He was the thing the other team hadn't planned for because they couldn't plan for a shape that kept changing. That was the whole of him. That had always been the whole of him.

He clocked her arrival as a change in the room before he found the avatar that caused it.

Off to his left, a knot of the Dead Hand frontline that had been grinding forward into Kip's wall suddenly wasn't — three of them peeled back a half-step together, the way a held formation flinches when something dangerous moves up behind it. A caster who'd been holding a cast broke it early and repositioned for no reason Marcus could see. The whole far side of the field reorganized itself around a gap that hadn't been a gap a second ago, the players nearest it making room without being told, the unconscious geometry of teammates clearing a lane for the one of them who didn't need it cleared and took it anyway. The fight didn't go quiet. It went *deferential*. Thirty people kept swinging and somewhere inside that a space opened up that everyone on her side was bending to leave for her.

Then he found her, because the gap had a source. She came up out of the back of the Dead Hand formation at a pace that read wrong against everyone else's — not faster, exactly, but *unhesitating*, no input wasted on deciding, an avatar moving like the decision had already been made somewhere off-screen and the body was only catching up to it. Lean, dark, economical. No flourish on the entry, no posturing, nothing the cameras would have wanted. Just a player crossing her own backline toward the fight with the total absence of doubt that, in this game, only ever meant one thing.

Something in him changed pitch — not fear, the other thing, the cold flat clarity he'd traded a normal life for. *There she is.* A season of her tape and he'd never once seen her on a live field with the trophy this close. He'd built the whole night around the question of whether 404 could survive her. Now the question had a body and it was already moving.

She didn't slow to read the fight. That was the part the tape never captured and the part he understood the instant he watched it happen: she didn't *survey*. She came in already knowing where she was going, the read done before she crossed her own backline, and the read was the kill that ended the night. Not the casters — the man keeping the casters alive.

She angled toward Brent.

It landed in Marcus's gut as a misjudgment for half a second — even she wouldn't walk a straight read into a braced support with a guardian standing near him — and then he understood, and the misread inverted into the cleanest move on the board. Kill the support and the swarm starves; their casters die without Brent's marks and the whole kill route folds inward. She had looked at thirty avatars and picked the one whose death won, and she was going for it on the shortest path there was, the way the best killer in the bracket spent her entry on the highest-value target and nothing else. Brent was the kill. Marcus was geometry between her and it — a body on a #25 guild, not a name she knew. She didn't look at him at all. She'd already spent her attention; there was none left over for the obstacle.

He stepped into her path anyway.

He dropped into Sentinel.

The change rolled up the avatar from the ground — that was the only way to read it, mass loading into the model frame by frame, plate thickening across the shoulders, the stance widening and rooting until the body he was driving stopped being something that moved and became something that *stayed.* The switch lit a cooldown on the form he'd just left; if he dropped Sentinel now, Arbiter was locked behind a timer he'd have to wait out before he could think in it again. He planted square in the gap between her approach and Brent, faced her dead on, and braced. Sentinel was the slow form, the heavy one, the one that couldn't chase and didn't need to, good for standing in one place and refusing to leave it, and he made himself the wall in front of the kill.

She hit it without slowing.

Her opener broke against the front of him — fast and committed, driving straight into the center of mass like she meant to go *through* the avatar rather than around it — and Sentinel ate it. All of it. The block held, the burst flared off his guard in a chain of blocked-damage flags and chunked nothing he couldn't spare, the hit indicators stacking and washing out against the planted bulk of him, none of it reaching Brent. For half a second the plan was a plan and Marcus let himself think she'd overcommitted, that he was finally going to be the player standing in the right place when the Dead Hand rogue read a room wrong.

Then she didn't reset.

A blocked assassin resets — peels off the wall, buys a half-step of distance, re-angles, comes again on an approach the wall can't cover. That was the textbook and she'd written enough of the textbook that he should have known. Instead she stayed *in* — closed the last inch to him rather than backing off it, came right up against the face of the wall where his block was strongest and his sightline narrowest, and used the one flaw a wall has. Sentinel faced one way and turned slow. A thing planted to stop a charge is a thing that can be walked around. She slid hard along his right side, past the edge of his guard before he could rotate the slow mass of the avatar to track her, and stepped through into the dead space behind him — and now his own armored bulk stood between *him* and Brent. The wall he'd raised to stop her had become the screen she was hiding behind to reach the kill.

Both doors were bad and he saw both in the same instant. Hold Sentinel and she walks the last steps to Brent while his slow form is still grinding around to face the wrong way. Drop Sentinel to chase her and the rotation locks him out of acting for the half-second it took a new form to come online — and that half-second was the one Brent would die in. She'd won the exchange before he picked which way to lose it, committed past the point a careful player takes, past where peeling off was free, the commitment itself the trap and the trap already shut behind her.

So he spent the half-second on purpose and made her spend something for it too.

He dropped Sentinel and the avatar shed its mass on the model — the heavy plate thinning out, the rooted stance breaking — and for that half-second the form was simply *gone*, neither shape, no guard up and no attack available, every input in his hands dead while the system loaded the next one. An exposed window, and he'd opened it on purpose. Then Predator came online and the avatar went light and fast and forward, and he spent the speed not on chasing her toward Brent — suicide to be quick in that gap — but on the space she'd just opened *behind* him. He closed it. Put the fast form's burst into the exit she'd carved and stood in it, ate the only clean angle she had to disengage.

He was a fraction too late to save Brent and just fast enough to save the rest. Her strike was already in flight when he turned, and it landed — full, committed, into the support who'd had no wall in front of him for one frame too long. Brent's health bar dropped like a stone, marks guttering out across the squad as he reeled, the medic's whole readout flashing into the red and staying there. Crippled, not dead. The kill she'd committed everything to didn't close — because the obstacle she'd walked around was now standing in her only door out, and finishing Brent meant turning her back on it. She'd spent everything to reach him and come up one body short of the one thing that made the spend worth it.

For the first time, she looked at him.

He felt it the way you feel a camera find you — the whole of her attention arriving at once, snapping off Brent and the casters and the swarm and onto the thing that had just refused to behave like geometry. He couldn't see a face. There was no face, just an avatar, lean and dark and economical. But he could read the *play*, and the play was no longer a player executing on the room. It was a player who had hit something unexpected and stopped to find out what it was.

Then the rest of the fight went quiet around them.

Not literally — Kip's wall still held somewhere, Seb's voice still ran under everything, *here, here, now here* — but it receded, the way the room recedes when one exchange becomes the only one that matters. She turned and squared on him, the kill abandoned, and now she was coming for the obstacle, because he was in the way and because he'd made himself worth the cost of looking at.

She opened the way she always opened. He could have hummed the rhythm — the false weight onto the lead foot, the half-beat hitch, then the real strike snapping out a tick later than the body promised, the opener that had ended more careers in this bracket than anyone else's whole kit. He'd watched it on tape three hundred times. So he wasn't where it expected him to be. He dropped Predator on the hitch and became Arbiter mid-step — and the switch cost him, it always cost him, that flicker of *neither-form* on the model, every input dead in his hands for the breath it took the new shape to load, the avatar committed to a quickness that was already gone. Her strike came out on schedule and went into the space where the fast form should have been standing. Arbiter wasn't standing there. Arbiter was half a step over and a hair slower, balanced, planted differently, reading — and her blade grazed plate instead of finding the gap it had aimed at. A graze flag bloomed across his vision, a thin chunk off his shields, the damage logged and survivable. He stayed up.

She reset.

He watched her do it, this time, watched the thing the tape never slowed down enough to show: she pulled the committed strike *back* through itself, killed the momentum she'd spent everything to build, re-set her feet a hand's width over, and bought the half-step of distance an assassin needs to throw a clean second look at a target that didn't die. No wasted motion in it. The reset was as committed as the attack — she didn't drift out and recover, she *snapped* to the new angle, already loaded, already coming. And she came again, cleaner, the opener adjusted for the slower form she'd just seen, the timing rebuilt around Arbiter's tempo.

He wasn't Arbiter anymore.

He'd already spent the switch — Sentinel, for the single exchange where her newly-careful commit needed something soft to land on and found stone instead — and the cost of it nearly killed him. The drop into Sentinel is the slow load; the mass comes onto the model heavy and unhurried, and for that half-second of building the heavy form the avatar could do *nothing*, not block, not move, the model caught mid-transition with every ability grayed out. Her strike was already in flight when he committed to the switch — the clean blow built for Arbiter's tempo, aimed at the gap where the balanced form's guard would be a fraction of a second from now. The strike came. The mass came too. The plate finished thickening across his front a hair before her timing expected stone there, the heavy bulk arriving into her path while she was still committed to a target that should have been a hair slower and a quarter-step lighter, and her blow that had been built to slip past Arbiter's guard drove flat into Sentinel's planted front and stopped, the way it had at the start. A fraction earlier and she'd have caught him neither-form and open. A fraction later and she'd have read the rotation off the silhouette and gone around it. She hit it in between, in the one frame the switch could have lived in, and the block flagged the whole burst absorbed — a chain of zeroes where a kill should have read.

And he saw her *register* it.

The next opener came in changed. Not the pattern off the tape — something else, something built live, in the moment, for *him*. She'd stopped running the book because the book didn't work on this body, and a player who throws out a season of muscle memory to improvise against the obstacle in front of her is a player who has decided the obstacle is worth improvising for. That was the thing the film never showed and the thing he understood now, planted in the open and trading reads with her: she had not respected him going in. To her he'd been geometry. And she was beginning to.

He could see it in the play and only in the play. In the way each commit got more careful and the care made her *more* dangerous, not less — closer to the edge she'd built her whole game on, the commit past the point a careful player bails, except now she was spending that commitment on him specifically and reading the result. In the half-second she gave to studying him between attempts, a half-second she spent on no one else in the room. In the plain fact that she had stopped trying to get *past* him and started trying to *beat* him — two different problems, and only one of them treated him as a player.

He held.

That was all he was doing and it was everything: holding the door against the best killer in the bracket, a player who was supposed to walk through bodies like his without breaking stride, and doing it the only way it could be done — by being four things in the space she'd planned to beat one, and paying for each of them. Because the rotation was never free. Every switch grayed his abilities out for a window he couldn't afford, a stretch where the avatar was neither shape and entirely open, an opening he was handing her each time and betting she couldn't find before the new form closed it. He was running four resource systems at once against a player who ran one with total commitment, and the math of that should have buried him — four sets of cooldowns and four reserve bars to hold in his head while reading a fight that moved at the speed hers did. It didn't bury him. It was simply what his game cost, the way her commitment cost her the option to bail. The load of it pressed on him, four ledgers and a clock on every one — and he met her, read for read, the two of them dead level and only one of them, for one more exchange, still finding that out.

For a span he stopped counting, there was nothing in the world but the next read. Then he rotated toward Oracle — the high-end form, the one he kept for the openings nobody else could see, the most expensive switch in his kit and the slowest to load — because she'd earned it, and because he wanted to find out, here, in the room, against her, whether he could —

The monitors along the upper wall came on.

He registered it the way you register the wrong note in a song you know — not as a thought, as a flinch. The monitors came on when a person patched in. That was the rule. That was the only time they lit. They were supposed to be dark through a match; the leader had told them himself, weeks ago, in some other scrim, that the power-down was a hard rule, that nobody on either side could touch the room once the doors were open.

The screens snowed over. All of them, at once, the whole upper bank, and the snow did not resolve into a face.

Serra had stopped. He could see it in the avatar — the stillness of a player who has just noticed the same wrong thing he has, the famous forward momentum simply *gone,* a dropped input from someone who did not drop inputs. Across the seam, thirty people who had been about to kill each other were all looking up at the same time.

The snow held. It did not become anyone. It did not become anything. It just stayed, a wall of it, filling the screens that were never supposed to be on, and under his hands the chair had gone strange, the cuffs no longer reading him so much as holding him, and the simple animal certainty he'd come here for — *nothing is steering this but the people in the room* — turned over in his chest and showed him its other side.

Something was steering it now.

He didn't know what. He only knew, with the flat sourceless conviction of the anomaly he'd left open on a notebook three days from a launch, that he was once again getting credit for work some other thing had reached in and done.
