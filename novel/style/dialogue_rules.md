# Dialogue Rules — Dominion Realm

> **Purpose:** How dialogue is written, formatted, and differentiated across characters. Includes practical tools for when dialogue feels stuck or flat. Cross-reference `prose_contract.md` and `style_examples.md`.

---

## The Core Problem This File Solves

Writing distinct character voices is hard. Writing dialogue that carries subtext instead of just information is hard. This file gives you concrete tools for both — not just principles, but procedures you can follow when a scene isn't working.

---

## The Fundamental Rule

People do not say what they mean when something else is available to say.

Dialogue is behavior. It is how characters manage the gap between what they feel and what they're willing to show. The gap is where character lives.

When a character finally says the direct thing — the true thing — it hits harder because they almost never do. Earn those moments.

---

## Tool 1: The Five Questions

Before writing any significant dialogue scene, answer these five questions. You don't need all five in every scene, but knowing the answers produces better scenes than discovering them mid-draft.

1. **What are they talking about on the surface?**
2. **What are they actually negotiating underneath?**
3. **What does each character want from this exchange?**
4. **What is each character unwilling to say?**
5. **What accidentally gets said that shouldn't?**

Example — Soren and Serra after surviving something together:

1. Surface: she's checking his shoulder injury
2. Underneath: whether she's staying or leaving, and neither of them wants to name it
3. Soren wants her to say she's staying. Serra wants him to not ask.
4. Soren won't say "please don't go." Serra won't say "I have to."
5. She asks how long he's been here. The question implies she's calculating how long she can stay. He hears it. Neither acknowledges it.

Now write the scene. The surface conversation runs. The underneath conversation runs simultaneously through what gets said, what doesn't, and where the pauses fall.

## Tool 2: Character Voice Profiles

Each character has a distinct spoken register. When a line of dialogue could belong to anyone, it belongs to no one. Use these profiles to differentiate.

### Marcus (Soren)
**How he talks:** Thinks while talking. Sentences sometimes revise themselves mid-delivery. Tends toward specificity — doesn't say "a long time," says the actual amount. Under pressure his speech compresses but stays complex. His humor is observational and sideways — the funny thing arrives unexpectedly because he noticed something true.

**What he doesn't do:** Announce his feelings directly. Say the romantic thing when he could say something functionally adjacent to it. Stop being funny when things get bad — the humor is a survival mechanism, not a mood.

**Tell when he's performing:** He sounds more certain than he is. A careful reader hears the seam — the slightly-too-clean sentence, the answer that came a beat too fast.

**From the original draft:** "Thanks for your endearing support, sasshole. I mean Seb." and "I want you to give me something to help me survive. Give me that, and release your hold from my mind, and you'll have yourself a deal." Both are classic Soren — the first is sideways humor while scared, the second is competence under pressure making the ask sound casual.

---

### Serra
**How she talks:** Direct and economical. Says the thing and stops. Processes privately, speaks the output. No revisions aloud. Warmth is in word choice and tone, not volume. When something matters she gets quieter, not louder. Almost never asks questions she doesn't already have an answer to.

**What she doesn't do:** Over-explain. Soften a hard thing by wrapping it in qualifications. Say "I'm leaving" when she can just start getting ready to leave.

**Tell when something is real:** She uses first names. Not "hey" — the actual name. When Serra says "Marcus," the sentence it's attached to matters.

**Differentiation from Soren:** He thinks out loud. She thinks in private and delivers the conclusion. He finds the angle; she already chose the angle before the conversation started.

---

### Seb
**How he talks:** Naturally warm and persuasive — drawing people toward him is his baseline. Frames his wants collectively: "what we need" rather than "what I want." Early Seb is genuine; late Seb is using this framing to convince himself as much as others. Under pressure he becomes more formal — the warmth pulls back, something more managed appears.

**What he doesn't do:** Lose his rhetorical skill. It is the last thing to go. Even when he's wrong, he sounds right.

**Arc tell:** Track when "we" becomes a rationalization. The moment Seb says "we need to do this" and means "I need to do this and need you to agree" — that's the turn.

---

### Mara
**How she talks:** Asks more questions than anyone. Genuine curiosity is her baseline. Technical precision mixed with warmth — can explain the biology of something while caring intensely about the person it's happening to. Sentences tend to finish fully. When scared or overcommitted she generates options aloud — she thinks by listing.

**What she doesn't do:** Dismiss the human cost while analyzing the mechanism. Pretend she doesn't care about the answer to a question she just asked.

**Differentiation from Serra:** Serra asks a question when she already knows the answer. Mara asks because she genuinely doesn't and wants to.

---

### Mathias
**How he talks:** Measured — pauses before speaking in a way others misread as hesitation. Uses ecological metaphors naturally, not deliberately. When something is urgent the measure drops and sentences get short. Asks questions that reframe rather than challenge.

**What he doesn't do:** Rush. Even when he should.

**Tell when something matters to him:** The sentences get shorter. The ecological metaphor disappears. He stops reframing and just says the thing.

---

### Brent
**How he talks:** Concrete and tool-oriented. Translates abstract concepts into physical terms. Dry humor that arrives without announcement — he doesn't set it up, he just says the true thing that happens to be funny. Occasionally blunt in a way that reads rude until you know him.

**What he doesn't do:** Engage with feelings directly. He'd rather fix the practical problem adjacent to the emotional one.

**Tell when something matters to him personally:** The sentences get unexpectedly longer. He doesn't translate it into physical terms. The abstract slips through.

---

### Ayla
**How she talks:** Complete thoughts that don't follow conversational rhythm. Asks the question underneath the question, not the surface one. Occasionally phrases things slightly wrong in ways that feel old rather than broken — idioms that have drifted, concepts that don't map cleanly to human expression. No filler. No "I mean" or "anyway." Every word is chosen.

**What she doesn't do:** Explain things in paragraphs. Give easy answers. Say more than she has to.

**From the original draft:** "No idiot, an inanimate machine is not spontaneously talking to you." and "Never say your true name" and "I am a celestial being." These are already her. Short. Dry. Slightly off-center. She doesn't elaborate unless pressed, and even then she only adds one more layer.

**Critical:** Do not let Ayla become an exposition machine. The moment she starts explaining the world at length, she stops being Ayla. Her knowledge is fragmented. Her answers create new questions. That is her function.

## Tool 3: The Line That Reveals Too Much

Characters occasionally say more than they intended. Not confessions — a single word choice, a question they shouldn't have asked, a pause in the wrong place. The other character notices. The reader notices. Nobody says anything about it.

This is one of the most powerful moves in dialogue and one of the hardest to write deliberately. The trick: pick a moment where a character has just made a decision privately that they haven't announced. Then find the single line they'd say that contains that decision without naming it.

Example: Serra has already decided she's leaving tomorrow. She doesn't say it. But when she asks "do you know how far Eriadne is from here?" — the question means something. Soren hears it. He doesn't ask what she's planning. She doesn't explain. The reader does the math.

## Tool 4: When Dialogue Is Flat — Diagnostic Questions

If a scene's dialogue isn't working, run through these:

**Is someone saying what they mean directly when they could show it through behavior?**
Fix: replace the statement with an action or an oblique comment that contains the same information.

**Do all the characters sound like the same person?**
Fix: go back to the voice profiles. Identify which character's natural register is being flattened. Rewrite their lines using their specific tells.

**Is the scene delivering information rather than doing something?**
Fix: identify what each character wants from this conversation. Make sure those wants are in conflict, even slightly. Wants in conflict generate pressure. Pressure generates dialogue that sounds like people rather than narration.

**Is the scene resolving too cleanly?**
Fix: leave one thing unaddressed. The best dialogue scenes end with something unsaid that the reader is still holding.

**Is Ayla explaining too much?**
Fix: cut her longest speech in half. Then in half again. Whatever she can't say in two or three lines, she doesn't say yet.

## Formatting

**Attribution:** Use "said" as the default. It disappears. Use action beats when possible — the action tells you who's speaking and what they're doing simultaneously.

**Action beats are characterization, not stage directions:**
- Wrong: *"Fine," she said, crossing her arms.* (generic, tells us nothing)
- Right: *"Fine." She set the cup down with more precision than it needed.* (specific, carries emotional information)

**Interruptions:**
- Hard cutoff mid-word: em-dash — *"I was just trying to—"*
- Trailing off: ellipsis — *"I don't know. Maybe..."*
- Talking past each other: handle through rhythm and action beats, not special formatting

**Internal thought:**
- Direct (a specific thought): italics — *That was a mistake.*
- Indirect (woven into narration): no formatting

**New speaker = new paragraph. Always. Even one word.**

---

## What Dialogue Never Does

- Delivers exposition characters would already know
- Names emotions directly when behavior can carry them
- Sounds identical across characters
- Wraps scenes up too cleanly
- Uses said-bookisms (he ejaculated, she opined, he intoned)
- Has Ayla explain things at length
